3.1.3 Customer Needs and Market Research

Why businesses need to understand customers

Marketing starts with understanding what customers need and want. A business that does not understand its customers risks making products that nobody buys, pricing them incorrectly, or promoting them in ways that do not reach the right audience. Market research reduces this risk — it does not eliminate it.

Market research

Definition
Market research is the process of gathering, analysing and interpreting information about a market, including information about the target market, customers, and competitors.

Primary research

Data collected first-hand by or for the business. Methods include surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, and test marketing. Primary research is specific to the business’s needs but is usually more expensive and time-consuming than secondary research.

Secondary research

Data that already exists, collected originally for another purpose. Sources include government statistics (ONS), trade journals, competitor reports, Mintel/Statista, and internal sales data. Secondary research is cheaper and quicker but may not be specific enough or may be out of date.

Quantitative vs qualitative

QuantitativeQualitative
Numerical data — how many, how much, percentagesNon-numerical data — opinions, feelings, reasons
Collected through surveys with closed questions, sales figures, website analyticsCollected through interviews, focus groups, open-ended questions
Can be statistically analysed, compared, and graphedProvides depth and insight into why customers behave as they do
Large samples needed for reliabilitySmaller samples — but harder to generalise

Confidence levels and confidence intervals

When a business surveys a sample of customers, it needs to know how confident it can be that the results reflect the whole market.

A confidence level tells you how sure you can be — for example, a 95% confidence level means that if the survey were repeated 100 times, the results would fall within the same range 95 times. A higher confidence level gives more certainty but usually requires a larger (and more expensive) sample.

A confidence interval is the range within which the true figure is likely to fall. For example, “42% of customers prefer brand X, with a confidence interval of ±3%” means the true figure is probably between 39% and 45%.

Note
100% confidence is never possible — there is always some uncertainty when using a sample rather than asking every single person in the market. Businesses must balance the desire for accuracy against the cost and time of larger samples.

Market mapping

Definition
Market mapping positions products or businesses on a grid using two variables that matter to customers (such as price vs quality, or age appeal vs style). It helps identify gaps in the market where customer needs are not being met, and shows how competitors are positioned relative to each other.

Market mapping is useful for identifying opportunities, but it simplifies a complex market into just two dimensions. A gap on the map does not necessarily mean a profitable opportunity — there may be a gap because there is no demand there.

Influences on the choice of market research

Risk — High-risk decisions (launching a new product, entering a new market) justify more thorough and expensive research. Low-risk decisions (small price adjustment) may need only internal sales data.

Cost — Primary research is expensive. Small businesses may rely more heavily on secondary research because they cannot afford large-scale surveys or focus groups.

Time — If a decision must be made quickly (a competitor has just cut prices), there may not be time for detailed primary research. Secondary data can be gathered faster.

Reliability — The value of research depends on how well it reflects reality. A badly designed survey with leading questions, or secondary data from five years ago, may lead to worse decisions than no research at all.

Common mistake
Students sometimes treat market research as if it guarantees good decisions. It does not. Research can be out of date, biased, or based on too small a sample. A good evaluation point is to question the reliability and relevance of the research a business has used.
3.1.3 Markets and Marketing Data

Types of market

TypeMeaningExample
B2BBusiness to business — one business sells to anotherA flour supplier selling to a bakery chain
B2CBusiness to consumer — a business sells directly to individual customersGymshark selling leggings to gym-goers online
C2CConsumer to consumer — individuals selling to each otherVinted, Depop, eBay marketplace sellers

The marketing approach differs significantly between these markets. B2B marketing tends to focus on building long-term relationships, emphasising reliability, cost-effectiveness, and product specifications. B2C marketing is more likely to focus on branding, emotional appeal, and reaching a large number of individual customers.

Goods vs services

A good is a physical, tangible product (a car, a pair of trainers). A service is an intangible product (insurance, a haircut, a Spotify subscription). Many businesses sell both — Apple sells goods (iPhones) and services (Apple Music, iCloud). The marketing of services is different because the customer cannot try before they buy in the same way, so trust, reviews, and reputation become more important.

Market conditions

Competition — How many competitors there are and how strong they are affects marketing decisions. In a highly competitive market, a business may need to spend more on promotion or compete more aggressively on price.

Competitive behaviour — What competitors are doing matters. If a rival launches a price war, a business must decide whether to match, undercut, or differentiate on quality instead. Competitive behaviour includes pricing decisions, advertising campaigns, new product launches, and distribution strategies.

Market growth — Is the overall market getting bigger or smaller? In a growing market, a business can increase sales even without taking market share from competitors. In a declining market, growth can only come from taking customers away from rivals.

Calculations

The specification requires you to calculate and interpret several marketing metrics. In the exam, you will be given the figures — your job is to select the right formula, calculate accurately, and then interpret what the result means for the business.

MetricFormulaWhat it tells you
Market sizeTotal value or volume of sales in a marketHow big the market is — measured by revenue (value) or units sold (volume)
Market shareSales of the business ÷ total market sales × 100The business’s proportion of the total market — a measure of competitiveness
Market growth(New market size − old market size) ÷ old market size × 100How fast the market is growing or shrinking — affects strategic decisions
Sales growth(New sales − old sales) ÷ old sales × 100How fast the business’s own sales are changing
Return on marketing spend(Revenue generated by marketing − marketing cost) ÷ marketing cost × 100How much return the business gets for each pound spent on marketing
Important
Always show your working in calculations. If you make an arithmetic error, the Own Figure Rule means you can still get marks for correctly interpreting your wrong answer. If you show no working, the examiner cannot award these marks.

Sales data

The spec says you should be able to analyse sales data per region, per product, and per store. This means comparing performance across different parts of the business. For example, if one region’s sales are growing while another’s are declining, a question might ask you to analyse why or recommend what action to take.

Interpreting data

Sales volume vs sales value — Sales volume is the number of units sold. Sales value is the total revenue. They can move in different directions — if a business cuts its price, volume may rise but value may fall (or stay the same). Always check which one the question is asking about.

Correlation — Correlation describes the relationship between two variables. A positive correlation means both variables move in the same direction (as advertising spending increases, sales increase). A negative correlation means they move in opposite directions (as price increases, quantity demanded falls). Correlation can be strong (the variables move closely together) or weak (the variables move in roughly the same direction but with lots of variation).

Common mistake
Correlation does not mean causation. Just because two things move together does not prove that one causes the other. A business might see a positive correlation between advertising spending and sales — but the sales increase could be caused by other factors (seasonal demand, competitor problems, economic growth). This is a strong evaluation point.

Index numbers, percentages and percentage changes

You may be given data as index numbers (a base year = 100) and asked to interpret what has happened since. For example, if the market size index went from 100 to 118, the market has grown by 18%. You should be comfortable calculating percentage changes and converting between percentages and raw numbers.

3.1.3 Demand, PED and YED

Influences on demand

Demand is the quantity of a product that customers are willing and able to buy at a given price. It is affected by:

Price — Generally, as price rises, demand falls (and vice versa). How much demand changes depends on the price elasticity of demand.

Income — As consumer incomes rise, demand for most products rises. But for some products (inferior goods), demand actually falls as incomes rise because consumers trade up to better alternatives.

Actions of competitors — If a competitor cuts its price or launches a new product, demand for your product may fall.

Marketing — Effective advertising, promotions, and branding can increase demand by making the product more desirable or visible.

Seasonal and other factors — Demand for ice cream rises in summer. Demand for certain goods rises around Christmas. External events (a pandemic, a recession) can shift demand dramatically.

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

Definition
Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive demand is to a change in price. It answers the question: if we change the price by a certain percentage, by what percentage will the quantity demanded change?

Formula

PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price

PED is always a negative number (because price and demand move in opposite directions), but by convention it is often expressed as a positive figure.

Interpreting PED

PED valueTermMeaningRevenue effect of a price increase
Greater than 1Price elasticDemand is very responsive to price changes — a small price change causes a larger proportionate change in demandRevenue falls — the drop in demand more than offsets the higher price
Less than 1Price inelasticDemand is not very responsive to price changes — a price change causes a smaller proportionate change in demandRevenue rises — demand does not fall enough to offset the higher price
Exactly 1Unit elasticThe percentage change in demand is exactly equal to the percentage change in priceRevenue stays the same

What makes demand more price inelastic?

Demand tends to be more inelastic (less responsive to price changes) when:

• The product is heavily branded — customers are loyal and less likely to switch

• There are few substitutes available — customers have limited alternatives

• The product represents a low proportion of income — a small price increase is barely noticed

• Someone else is paying — the person choosing the product is not the person paying for it

• In the short term — customers take time to find alternatives; in the long term demand tends to become more elastic

Important link
An increase in revenue does not guarantee an increase in profits. If a business cuts its price and volume rises (elastic demand), revenue may increase — but the extra volume requires extra production, which increases variable costs. Whether profits rise depends on whether the extra revenue exceeds the extra costs. This is a strong evaluation point.

PED and business decisions

If demand is elastic, a business should consider lowering its price to increase revenue. If demand is inelastic, a business should consider raising its price to increase revenue. However, this is a starting point, not the whole answer — the business must also consider the effect on costs, brand image, competitor reactions, and long-term customer relationships.

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

Definition
Income elasticity of demand (YED) measures how responsive demand is to a change in consumer income. It answers the question: if incomes change by a certain percentage, by what percentage will the quantity demanded change?

Formula

YED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in income

Interpreting YED

YED valueType of goodMeaningExample
Positive (> 0)Normal goodDemand rises as income risesBranded clothing, holidays, dining out
Negative (< 0)Inferior goodDemand falls as income rises — consumers switch to better alternativesValue-range supermarket products, budget airlines (in some contexts)
Positive and > 1Income elastic (luxury)Demand rises by a greater proportion than the income increaseSports cars, designer handbags, fine dining
Positive and < 1Income inelastic (necessity)Demand rises by a smaller proportion than the income increaseBread, toothpaste, basic clothing

YED and business decisions

Understanding YED helps businesses plan for changes in the economy. During a recession (falling incomes), a business selling income-elastic luxury goods should expect a significant drop in demand and may need to adjust its product range, pricing, or marketing. A business selling income-inelastic necessities will be less affected.

A business selling inferior goods may actually benefit from a recession, as consumers trade down from premium alternatives. However, as the economy recovers, these businesses should expect demand to fall again.

Common mistake
PED and YED are not the same thing. PED measures responsiveness to price changes. YED measures responsiveness to income changes. A product can be price inelastic but income elastic. Students often confuse these in the exam. Make sure you are answering the right one.
3.1.3 Target Market and Segmentation

Market segmentation

Definition
Market segmentation is the process of dividing a market into distinct groups of customers who share similar characteristics or needs. The business then targets one or more of these segments with a tailored marketing mix.

Bases for segmentation

TypeWhat it involvesExample
DemographicAge, gender, income, occupation, education, family sizeGymshark targets 18–25-year-olds interested in fitness
GeographicLocation — country, region, urban/rural, climateA business selling winter clothing focuses on northern European markets
PsychographicLifestyle, values, attitudes, personalityPatagonia targets environmentally conscious outdoor enthusiasts
BehaviouralBuying habits, brand loyalty, usage rate, benefits soughtStarbucks Rewards targets frequent customers with personalised offers

Niche vs mass markets

Niche marketMass market
TargetSmall, specific segment with particular needsThe whole market or a very large segment
CompetitionOften less competition, but limited market sizeHighly competitive, but very large potential sales
PriceOften higher prices because of specialisationOften lower prices to appeal to a broad audience
ExamplesRolls-Royce, Lush, Rapha cycling clothingCoca-Cola, Samsung, Primark

Adapting the marketing mix for the target market

Once a business has identified its target market, it adjusts its marketing mix (product, price, place, promotion) to match the needs and characteristics of that segment. A luxury brand targeting high-income consumers will use premium pricing, selective distribution, and aspirational advertising. A mass-market retailer targeting price-sensitive families will use competitive pricing, wide distribution, and value-focused promotion.

3.1.3 Marketing Objectives and Planning

Marketing objectives

Marketing objectives are specific, measurable targets that the marketing function aims to achieve. They should be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time-specific).

The specification lists the following marketing objectives:

ObjectiveWhat it means
Lead generationAttracting potential customers who express interest in the product — signing up to a newsletter, filling in a contact form, requesting a quote
SalesIncreasing the volume or value of sales — the most directly measurable marketing outcome
Market shareIncreasing the business’s proportion of total market sales — a measure of competitiveness relative to rivals
Brand awarenessMaking more people aware of the brand — important for new products or businesses entering new markets
Customer retentionKeeping existing customers and encouraging repeat purchases — often cheaper than acquiring new ones
EngagementBuilding interaction between customers and the brand — likes, shares, comments, app usage, community involvement
ConversionTurning interest into action — the percentage of website visitors who actually make a purchase, or leads who become customers

Marketing budget

The marketing budget is the amount of money allocated to marketing activities. Setting the budget involves trade-offs — spending more on marketing may increase sales, but it also increases costs and reduces short-term profits. The budget is influenced by the business’s overall objectives, the level of competition, and the stage of the product lifecycle.

Demand changes

Businesses must plan for changes in demand — whether demand is rising, falling, cyclical (following the economic cycle), or seasonal (following predictable patterns within the year). Planning for these changes affects staffing, stock levels, cash flow, and the marketing approach.

Interrelationship with other functions

Marketing does not operate in isolation. Marketing decisions affect and are affected by the other functions of business:

• Finance — Marketing needs a budget. Finance must fund marketing activities. Sales targets feed into revenue forecasts and cash flow projections.

• Operations — If marketing generates demand, operations must be able to meet it. A successful promotion is pointless if the business cannot supply the product.

• People — Marketing campaigns may require new skills (digital marketing, data analytics). Sales targets affect how sales teams are managed and rewarded.

Note
The spec explicitly asks about the interrelationship between marketing and other functional areas. This is a natural evaluation and synoptic point — marketing decisions that look good in isolation may create problems elsewhere in the business.

Competitiveness

Effective marketing contributes to competitiveness by differentiating the business from rivals, building brand loyalty, and attracting new customers. But competitiveness also depends on the product itself, pricing, distribution, and the effectiveness of operations and people management. Marketing alone is not enough.

3.1.3 Marketing Mix: Product

The product lifecycle

The product lifecycle describes the stages a product goes through from initial development to eventual decline.

StageWhat happensMarketing implications
DevelopmentThe product is being designed, tested, and prepared for launch. No revenue — only costs.Market research, test marketing, refining the concept. Cash flow is negative.
IntroductionThe product is launched. Sales grow slowly as customers become aware of it.Heavy promotion needed to build awareness. Pricing may be high (skimming) or low (penetration). Distribution may be limited initially.
GrowthSales rise rapidly. The product gains market acceptance. Competitors may enter.Widen distribution. Build brand loyalty before competitors establish themselves. Revenue grows but marketing costs remain high.
MaturitySales growth slows and levels off. The market is saturated. Competition is intense.Defend market share. Consider extension strategies. Focus on cost control and efficiency. Profits are often highest here.
DeclineSales fall. The product becomes outdated or customer preferences change.Decide whether to revive the product (extension strategy), harvest remaining profits with minimal investment, or withdraw it.

Extension strategies

An extension strategy attempts to extend the maturity phase and prevent decline. The specification lists four:

• Advertising — New campaigns to re-energise interest in the product

• Rebranding — Updating the product’s image, packaging, or positioning

• Lowering price — Making the product more accessible to price-sensitive customers

• Adapting the product — Adding new features, flavours, sizes, or variations

Boston Matrix

The Boston Matrix classifies a business’s products based on their market share and the rate of market growth.

High market shareLow market share
High market growthStars — Leading products in growing markets. Generate high revenue but need continued investment. May become cash cows if market growth slows.Question marks — Products in growing markets but with low share. Require heavy investment to grow share, or may be dropped if they cannot compete.
Low market growthCash cows — Established products with high share in mature markets. Generate strong cash flow with little investment needed. Fund other products.Dogs — Low share in low-growth markets. May break even or make a small loss. Usually candidates for divestment unless they serve a strategic purpose.
How to use the Boston Matrix
The Boston Matrix helps a business manage its product portfolio. A healthy business needs a balance — cash cows to fund investment in question marks, stars to drive future growth, and a plan for dogs. In the exam, apply it to the business in the case study: identify where each product sits and what this means for resource allocation.

New product development (NPD)

New product development is the process of bringing a new product to market. It is essential for long-term survival because existing products eventually decline. However, NPD is risky — many new products fail. The costs of development can be substantial, and there is no guarantee that customers will want the new product.

NPD links to the product lifecycle (every new product starts at the development stage), the Boston Matrix (a new product may begin as a question mark), and marketing objectives (NPD may be driven by a desire to increase market share or enter new segments).

Example
Apple’s Vision Pro represents a major NPD investment. The development costs were estimated at over $3 billion. In the SAM Paper 1, the 15-mark question asks students to evaluate which regional market Apple should launch Vision Pro in to maximise ROI — illustrating how NPD is linked to marketing strategy, financial management, and international marketing.
3.1.3 Marketing Mix: Price

Influences on pricing decisions

The specification lists a number of factors that influence pricing:

Marketing objectives — A business aiming for market share may price low. A business aiming for premium positioning may price high.

Target market — Price must match what the target customer is willing and able to pay.

Level of demand — High demand may support higher prices. Weak demand may require price cuts.

PED — If demand is elastic, even a small price rise will cause a significant drop in sales. If demand is inelastic, the business has more pricing power.

Costs — The price must at least cover costs in the long term, or the business will make a loss.

Competitors — In a competitive market, pricing is constrained by what rivals charge.

Marketing mix — Price must be consistent with the rest of the mix. A premium product with heavy advertising and selective distribution should carry a premium price.

Impact of price on sales volume, revenue and profit margins

A price increase will typically reduce sales volume but may increase revenue (if demand is inelastic) and improve profit margins per unit. A price decrease will typically increase sales volume but may reduce revenue (if demand is inelastic) and compress margins. The net effect on total profit depends on the interaction between price, volume, and costs.

Pricing methods

MethodHow it worksWhen it is used
Cost-basedSet a price by adding a mark-up to the cost of productionSimple and ensures costs are covered. Common in manufacturing and retail.
Demand-basedSet a price based on what customers are willing to payUsed when the business has strong brand power or unique products.
Penetration pricingSet a low initial price to attract customers and gain market share, then raise prices laterNew products entering competitive markets. Requires volume sales to cover costs.
Price skimmingSet a high initial price and lower it over time as the market maturesNew, innovative products with few substitutes (e.g. new technology). Early adopters pay a premium.
Dynamic pricingPrices change in real time based on demand, competition, or timeAirlines, hotels, ride-sharing apps (Uber surge pricing), event tickets.
Competition-basedSet prices in response to what competitors chargeMarkets with similar products where customers compare prices easily.
Premium pricingSet a permanently high price to reflect quality and exclusivityLuxury brands — Rolex, Apple, Louis Vuitton. Price is part of the brand.
Going rateSet the price at the same level as competitorsCommodity-type products where differentiation is difficult.
Discount pricingOffer reductions from the normal price to encourage purchaseSales, bulk buy offers, seasonal clearance, introductory offers.
Example
In the SAM Paper 1 Q2.1, students are asked to analyse how Apple’s high pricing strategy could affect its revenue. Apple uses premium pricing — the iPhone is significantly more expensive than most Android alternatives. The high price reinforces the brand’s premium positioning and, because Apple has strong brand loyalty (inelastic demand), the higher price does not reduce demand proportionally — so revenue rises.
3.1.3 Marketing Mix: Distribution and Place

Distribution channels

A distribution channel is the route a product takes from the manufacturer to the customer. There are several options:

Direct distribution

The business sells directly to the customer with no intermediary. This includes selling through the business’s own website (e-commerce), company-owned stores, or direct sales teams. Direct distribution gives the business more control over price, branding, and the customer experience, and it avoids sharing profit margins with intermediaries. However, it requires the business to manage its own logistics, customer service, and fulfilment.

Intermediaries

Wholesalers buy in bulk from manufacturers and sell in smaller quantities to retailers. They provide storage, break bulk, and reduce the number of transactions the manufacturer needs to manage.

Retailers sell directly to consumers. They provide a physical or online location where customers can browse, compare, and purchase products.

Multi-channel distribution

Using more than one distribution channel to reach customers — for example, selling through both a company website and through retailers, or through physical stores and an app. Multi-channel distribution increases the number of customers reached but can create channel conflict (retailers may resent the business competing with them through direct sales) and adds complexity to logistics and pricing.

Example
Gymshark built its business almost entirely through direct e-commerce, avoiding high street retailers. This gave it control over pricing and customer data, and avoided the margin that retailers would take. More recently, it has opened physical stores — a move towards multi-channel distribution.
3.1.3 Marketing Mix: Promotional Mix

Elements of the promotional mix

Advertising

Paid communication through media channels — TV, radio, print, online display ads, social media ads. The choice of media depends on the target audience, the budget, and the message. TV advertising reaches a large audience but is expensive. Social media advertising is cheaper and more precisely targeted but may reach fewer people.

Social media and influencers

Promoting through social media platforms (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook) and partnerships with influencers who endorse the product to their followers. This is particularly effective for reaching younger consumers and for building brand engagement, but it depends on finding the right influencers whose audience matches the target market.

Sales promotions

Short-term incentives to encourage purchase — discounts, buy-one-get-one-free, loyalty points, competitions, free samples. Sales promotions can boost short-term sales but can damage the brand if overused (customers learn to wait for the sale) and erode profit margins.

Sponsorship

Paying to be associated with an event, team, or individual — for example, Nike sponsoring athletes or Red Bull sponsoring extreme sports events. Sponsorship builds brand awareness and association with positive values but is expensive and the return on investment can be difficult to measure.

Personal selling

Face-to-face or direct contact between a salesperson and a customer. This includes account managers, sales teams, and client relationship managers. Personal selling is particularly important in B2B markets and for high-value products where the customer needs information, reassurance, or a tailored solution. It is expensive per contact but effective for building long-term relationships.

Brand, brand loyalty and own-label brands

Definition
A brand is a name, logo, design, or reputation that distinguishes one product from another. Brand loyalty is when customers repeatedly choose a particular brand over its competitors, even when alternatives are available.

Strong brands make demand more price inelastic (customers pay more for the brand) and reduce marketing costs over time (loyal customers do not need to be re-acquired). Own-label brands (also called private-label or store brands) are products sold under the retailer’s own name rather than the manufacturer’s — for example, Tesco Finest, Sainsbury’s Taste the Difference. These give the retailer more control over pricing and margins.

Influences on the promotional mix

The choice of promotional tools depends on: the target audience (younger audiences respond better to social media), the budget (TV is expensive; social media is relatively cheap), the type of product (complex B2B products may need personal selling), the stage in the product lifecycle (new products need heavy promotion; mature products may need less), and the actions of competitors.

Example
In the SAM Paper 1 Q1.3, students are asked to assess whether Gymshark should increase spending on influencers to improve profits. Gymshark built its entire brand through influencer marketing rather than traditional advertising — it had 18 million social media followers and used influencer partnerships on Instagram and TikTok to reach its core 18–24 target market.
3.1.3 Digital Technology and Marketing

Digital marketing tools

The specification lists a range of digital technologies that affect marketing. These are not separate topics — they are tools that a business uses to deliver its marketing strategy more effectively.

AI in marketing

Artificial intelligence is used for personalised product recommendations (Amazon, Netflix), chatbots for customer service, predictive analytics to forecast demand, automated ad targeting, and content generation. AI helps businesses respond faster to customer behaviour and reduces costs, but raises questions about privacy and the quality of human interaction.

Social media campaigns

Planned promotional activity across social media platforms, designed to achieve a specific marketing objective (brand awareness, engagement, conversions). Effective campaigns combine organic content with paid advertising, and may involve user-generated content, hashtags, or challenges.

Content marketing

Creating valuable, relevant content (blogs, videos, podcasts, infographics) to attract and retain an audience, rather than directly promoting a product. The idea is that by providing useful content, the business builds trust and keeps the brand visible. Content marketing works best as a long-term strategy.

SEO and PPC

Search Engine Optimisation (SEO) — Improving a website’s ranking in organic (unpaid) search results by using relevant keywords, quality content, and technical best practice. SEO is “free” in the sense that you do not pay for clicks, but it takes time and expertise.

Pay Per Click (PPC) — Paying for advertising that appears in search results or on other websites. The business pays each time someone clicks the ad. PPC delivers immediate visibility but costs can escalate quickly if not managed carefully.

Cookies

Small data files stored on a user’s device that track browsing behaviour. Cookies allow businesses to personalise advertising, track user journeys, and retarget people who have visited their website. However, privacy regulations (GDPR) now require consent, and major browsers are restricting third-party cookies.

Measures of digital marketing success

MetricWhat it measures
Search performanceWhere the business appears in search results for relevant terms
Click-through rate (CTR)The percentage of people who see an ad or link and click on it
Time on siteHow long visitors spend on the website — a measure of engagement and content quality
Conversion rateThe percentage of visitors who take a desired action (purchase, sign-up, enquiry)
SubscribersThe number of people who sign up for a mailing list, channel, or service
Advertising revenueIncome generated from selling advertising space (relevant for media businesses)

CRM (Customer Relationship Management)

CRM systems collect and analyse customer data to improve relationships and increase sales. Key functions include:

Customer acquisition — Identifying and attracting new customers through targeted marketing.

Retention — Keeping existing customers through personalised communication, loyalty programmes, and responsive service.

Lead generation — Identifying potential customers and managing the process of converting them into buyers.

Pipeline management — Tracking where each customer or lead is in the buying process, from initial interest to purchase.

Note
The importance of CRM varies by context. In B2B markets, where each customer relationship may be worth millions, CRM is critical. For smaller businesses or those in highly competitive markets, CRM helps them compete with larger rivals by being more responsive and personalised.

Data analytics

Using data to make better marketing decisions. The spec lists five applications:

Segmentation — Using data to identify and target customer segments more precisely.

Campaign tracking — Measuring the performance of marketing campaigns in real time and adjusting them.

Personalisation — Tailoring the product, price, or message to individual customers based on their behaviour and preferences.

Predictions — Using historical data to forecast future demand, trends, or customer behaviour.

Dynamic pricing — Adjusting prices in real time based on demand, competition, time, or customer data.

3.1.3 International Marketing A-level only

Purpose and value

Selling internationally allows a business to access larger markets, spread risk across different economies, and benefit from economies of scale. It can also extend the product lifecycle — a product in decline in one market may find new customers in another.

Challenges

Language barriers — Marketing materials, packaging, and customer service must be adapted for different languages. Translation errors can damage the brand.

Cultural and religious differences — What works in one market may not work in another. Colours, images, humour, and product features may have different meanings or acceptability in different cultures.

Legal and regulatory differences — Different countries have different rules on advertising, product standards, labelling, and data protection.

Logistics and distribution — Getting the product to customers in another country involves additional costs, longer supply chains, and potential trade barriers (tariffs, quotas).

Exchange rate fluctuations — Changes in currency values affect the price competitiveness of exports and the cost of importing materials.

Adapting the marketing mix for international markets

A business must decide how much to standardise its marketing mix across all markets (cheaper, consistent brand image) versus how much to adapt it for local markets (better fit with local customer needs). This is a trade-off between efficiency and effectiveness.

Example
In the SAM Paper 1 Q2.5, students evaluate which regional market Apple should launch Vision Pro in. This combines international marketing with ROI calculations — the business must consider not just the size of the potential market but the costs of adapting marketing, dealing with regulatory requirements, and managing logistics in each region.
3.1.3 Ethical Issues in Marketing A-level only

Ethical dilemmas in marketing

The specification asks about ethical issues relating to product, promotional, and pricing decisions.

Product

Should a business sell a product it knows is harmful (high-sugar food aimed at children, gambling apps)? Should it use environmentally damaging materials to keep costs low? Should planned obsolescence (designing products to become outdated quickly) be acceptable?

Promotion

Is it ethical to target advertising at vulnerable groups (children, elderly people)? Should businesses use misleading claims or manipulative emotional advertising? What about the ethics of collecting and using customer data for targeted advertising?

Pricing

Is it ethical to charge very high prices for essential goods (medicines, water)? Is dynamic pricing fair when it means some customers pay significantly more than others for the same product? Is it ethical to use predatory pricing to drive competitors out of the market?

Common mistake
Ethical and legal are not the same thing. Something can be legal but unethical (targeting junk food advertising at children is legal in some countries but many people consider it unethical). Something can be ethical but costly (switching to sustainable packaging may be the right thing to do but increases costs). The exam is interested in the tension between doing the right thing and doing the profitable thing.

Warning Common Mistakes in 3.1.3
Mistakes to avoid
1. Saying “marketing = advertising” — Marketing is much broader than advertising. It includes market research, product development, pricing, distribution, and branding. Advertising is just one element of the promotional mix.

2. Confusing PED and YED — PED measures responsiveness to price changes. YED measures responsiveness to income changes. They use different formulas and answer different questions. Check which one the question is asking about.

3. Assuming higher revenue means higher profit — A price cut may increase revenue (if demand is elastic) but the extra volume requires extra costs. Profit depends on the relationship between revenue and total costs, not revenue alone.

4. Treating correlation as causation — Just because two things move together does not mean one causes the other. Always consider other possible explanations.

5. Being one-sided about ethical issues — Ethical questions require you to explore the tension between doing the right thing and doing the profitable thing. A one-sided answer that says “the business should always be ethical” without considering the costs and trade-offs will not score well on evaluation.

6. Listing marketing mix elements without analysis — A question about pricing strategy does not want a list of all nine pricing methods. Pick the most relevant one and analyse why it is appropriate for this business in this context.

7. Ignoring the case study data — In PED and YED questions, you will often be given figures. Use them. Calculate with them. Refer to them throughout your answer. Generic answers about PED without using the data provided will not access top marks.

8. Confusing ethical and legal — Something can be legal but unethical (or ethical but illegal). The exam is interested in the grey area where the law allows something but doing it may still be questionable.
Exam How It Comes Up — Question Types and Command Words

6-mark Analyse questions

You will be asked to analyse one factor, one influence, or one reason — applied to the case study business. Pick one point, show knowledge (AO1), apply to the context (AO2), and build a chain of reasoning to the endpoint of the question (AO3). Do not try to cover multiple points.

Examples from the SAMs:

• “Analyse how the strength of Gymshark’s brand could affect the PED for its products”

• “Analyse how Apple’s high pricing strategy could affect its revenue”

9-mark Assess questions

You will be asked to weigh up whether a business should take a particular action or whether a particular factor is significant. Structure: FOR → AGAINST → weighted judgement. Use case study data including manipulated data (AO2), build chains of analysis with connectives (AO3), and reach a clear judgement that answers the question (AO4).

Examples from the SAMs:

• “Assess whether increasing spending on influencers would lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd”

• “Assess whether Apple should spend more on market research to improve profitability”

• “Assess whether new product development is the best way for Apple to increase market share”

15-mark Evaluate questions

You will be asked to evaluate two courses of action and recommend which is better. This is the most demanding question type and carries the most AO4 marks (5). Both options must be analysed and evaluated, with a clear, justified recommendation at the end.

Example from the SAMs:

• “Evaluate which regional market Apple should launch Vision Pro in to maximise ROI” (15m quantitative)

Note on quantitative questions
When a question asks you to perform a calculation (PED, market share, return on marketing spend, ROI), 6 of the marks are for quantitative skills: AO1 (2 marks) for knowing the formula and selecting the right numbers, AO2 (2 marks) for performing the calculation correctly, AO3 (1 mark) for interpreting the result, and AO4 (1 mark) for using the result in your judgement. Always show your working — the Own Figure Rule means you can still get marks for correctly interpreting an incorrect calculation.

6-mark Analyse: “Analyse how the strength of Gymshark’s brand could affect the PED for its products.” [6 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q1.2

Model answer

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive demand is to a change in price. A product with strong brand loyalty tends to have a more price inelastic demand, meaning a change in price causes a proportionally smaller change in the quantity demanded. Gymshark has built a powerful brand among younger gym-goers, with 18 million social media followers and a reputation for high-quality fitness clothing endorsed by leading influencers on Instagram and TikTok. This means that customers who identify strongly with the Gymshark brand are less likely to switch to cheaper alternatives such as Primark activewear or unbranded gym clothing when Gymshark raises its prices, because part of what they are paying for is the brand identity and the association with the fitness community that Gymshark represents. As a result, Gymshark is able to charge premium prices — its leggings retail at £40–£50, significantly above unbranded equivalents — without experiencing a proportionate drop in sales volume. This could lead to higher revenue for each unit sold, which shows that Gymshark’s strong brand makes its demand more price inelastic, giving the business greater pricing power and the ability to increase revenue through higher prices without losing a proportionate number of customers.

What makes this work
Knowledge of PED linked immediately to brand loyalty. Applied to Gymshark’s specific brand and target market throughout — not generic. The chain of reasoning uses connectives (“this means that”, “because”, “as a result”, “this could lead to”, “which shows that”) and reaches the endpoint: brand strength makes PED more inelastic, giving the business pricing power.

9-mark Assess: “Assess whether Apple should spend more on market research to improve profitability.” [9 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q2.3

Structure — FOR → AGAINST → DECIDE
Each argument paragraph needs: case study data including manipulated data woven throughout → at least five chains of analysis using connectives (this means that, as a result, therefore, this could lead to, this is because) reaching the endpoint → business models or theories applied where relevant.

The weighted judgement follows a fixed formula: Overall… → Although… carries greater weight because… → However, this ultimately depends on… → Therefore [final sentence answering the question in the words of the question].

Step 1 — Argument FOR (Apple should spend more on market research)

Argument for

On one hand, one could argue that Apple should spend more on market research to improve profitability. Market research involves gathering and analysing information about customers, competitors and market conditions. More detailed research can help a business understand what customers want, reducing the risk of launching products that fail. Apple invests heavily in new product development — the Vision Pro alone reportedly cost over $3 billion to develop — and if market research can improve the success rate of these investments, even a small improvement could generate significant returns. This means that spending more on research before committing billions to development would help Apple identify potential problems with a product early, which could reduce the risk of launching a product that fails to achieve sufficient market demand. As a result, the money saved from avoiding one failed product launch would likely outweigh the cost of the additional research many times over, therefore improving profitability by reducing wasted development expenditure. Apple’s operating profit margin is approximately 30%, which is high compared to most technology companies, and this means that even a modest improvement in the hit rate of new product launches would compound into significant profit gains given the scale of Apple’s operations. In terms of the product lifecycle, better research at the development stage could lead to products that move more quickly through the introduction phase and reach the profitable growth and maturity stages sooner.

Step 2 — Argument AGAINST (Apple should not spend more on market research)

Argument against

On the other hand, one might argue that Apple should not spend more on market research to improve profitability. Market research has limitations — it can only tell a business what customers think they want today, and for genuinely innovative products, customers may not be able to articulate what they want until they see it. Apple has a track record of creating entirely new product categories — the iPod, iPhone, iPad, and Apple Watch were all products that no market research predicted customers would want in the quantities they actually bought. This means that for a company whose competitive advantage depends on innovation and surprise, spending more on market research might lead to safer, more incremental products, which could reduce the boldness of Apple’s product development and weaken its brand positioning as the most innovative technology company. As a result, the profitability improvement from better research might be offset by a gradual decline in Apple’s ability to command premium prices, because Apple’s premium pricing — an iPhone 15 Pro Max retails at around £1,199, far above most Android alternatives — depends on customers believing that Apple offers something genuinely different and innovative. This shows that if market research pushes Apple towards products that customers already know they want rather than products they did not know they needed, the opportunity cost could be a loss of the innovation premium that underpins Apple’s high margins. There is also the risk of diminishing returns — Apple already conducts extensive research, and additional spending may produce little extra insight while adding to costs.

Step 3 — Weighted judgement

Weighted judgement

Overall, spending more on market research could improve Apple’s profitability, but the extent of the improvement depends on how the research is used. Although the risk of losing Apple’s innovation edge is real, the argument for more research carries greater weight because the scale of Apple’s development investments — billions of dollars per product — means that even a small improvement in decision-making at the research stage could prevent a costly failure and save significantly more than the research itself costs. However, this ultimately depends on how the research is integrated into Apple’s development process — if it is used to refine and de-risk bold ideas rather than replace them with safe ones, it can complement Apple’s innovation culture rather than undermine it. Therefore, Apple should spend more on market research to improve profitability, provided it uses the research to validate and improve innovative products rather than to decide what to innovate.

What makes these answers work
6-mark: One clear chain of reasoning from brand strength → inelastic PED → pricing power → revenue. Applied to Gymshark throughout with specific data.

9-mark: FOR and AGAINST both use case study data. Each paragraph has multiple chains of analysis with connectives. The weighted judgement weighs up both sides and comes down on one, with a condition (“provided”). The final sentence answers the question in its own words.

Context Real-World Examples for 3.1.3
Real-world examples
PED and brand loyalty: Apple’s iPhones are significantly more expensive than most Android alternatives, yet Apple maintains around 27% of the global smartphone market by volume and over 50% by profit. The strong brand makes demand highly price inelastic — a price increase does not cause a proportionate fall in demand.

Influencer marketing: Gymshark grew from a garage business to a $1 billion valuation by using influencer marketing on Instagram and TikTok instead of traditional advertising. Ben Francis identified that his target market (18–24-year-old gym-goers) trusted influencers more than TV adverts, and built the entire brand strategy around this insight.

Product lifecycle and extension strategies: Coca-Cola has used extension strategies (Diet Coke, Coke Zero, flavour variations, rebranding) to keep its core product in the maturity stage for decades. Without these extensions, the original product would likely have entered decline.

Boston Matrix: Unilever manages a portfolio of hundreds of brands. Dove (high share, mature market) is a cash cow. A newly acquired brand in a growing market might be a question mark. Unilever uses cash cow profits to fund investment in question marks and stars.

Market segmentation: Netflix uses behavioural and psychographic segmentation to personalise recommendations for each subscriber. The platform has thousands of micro-genres (e.g. “critically acclaimed emotional dramas”) based on viewing data, which improves engagement and retention.

Dynamic pricing: Uber uses surge pricing to adjust fares based on real-time demand. When demand exceeds supply (rush hour, bad weather, events), prices increase. This is an example of dynamic pricing driven by data analytics.

International marketing: McDonald’s adapts its menu for local markets — the McSpicy Paneer in India, the Teriyaki Burger in Japan, the McArabia in the Middle East. The core brand is standardised globally, but the product element of the marketing mix is adapted for cultural and religious preferences.

Ethical marketing: The fast fashion industry (SHEIN, Boohoo) has faced criticism for promoting overconsumption through aggressive social media marketing, using micro-influencers to create a constant cycle of new trends. The marketing is effective at driving sales but raises ethical questions about sustainability and environmental impact.

Summary Exam Reminders for 3.1.3
Things to remember
1. Marketing is not just advertising. It includes research, product development, pricing, distribution, and branding. Do not reduce it to one element.

2. PED formula: percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price. Elastic (> 1) means a price cut raises revenue. Inelastic (< 1) means a price rise raises revenue. But higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit.

3. YED formula: percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in income. Positive YED = normal good. Negative YED = inferior good. YED > 1 = income elastic (luxury). YED < 1 = income inelastic (necessity).

4. PED and YED are different things. Do not confuse them. Check which one the question is asking about.

5. Correlation does not mean causation. If two variables move together, it does not prove one causes the other. This is a strong evaluation point.

6. Product lifecycle: development → introduction → growth → maturity → decline. Extension strategies try to prevent decline. The Boston Matrix helps manage the product portfolio.

7. There are nine pricing methods on the spec. Do not list them all. Pick the one most relevant to the case study and analyse why it is appropriate.

8. SMART = Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time-specific. Not “Achievable”.

9. Always show your working in calculations. The Own Figure Rule means you get marks for correctly interpreting a wrong calculation, but only if the examiner can see what you did.

10. In 6-mark analyse questions, focus on one point with a clear chain of reasoning to the endpoint. In 9-mark assess questions, use FOR → AGAINST → weighted judgement. In 15-mark evaluate questions, analyse and evaluate two options and recommend which is better.

11. Apply the case study throughout. Use names, figures, and specific details from the stimulus material. Manipulate the data where possible — do not just repeat it. Generic answers cannot access top marks.

12. International marketing and ethical issues in marketing are A-level only topics — they will not appear on the AS paper.
