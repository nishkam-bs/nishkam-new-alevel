FOUNDATION Core calculation methods

These are not numbered formulas in Annex 7, but they underpin almost every quantitative question on the paper. Get these right first.

Percentage change
Percentage change (%) = New value − Original valueOriginal value × 100
Rules
  • Always divide by the original (starting) value, not the new one.
  • A negative answer means a decrease; a positive answer means an increase.
  • Show your working — even a simple percentage change earns method marks.
Common traps
  • Dividing by the new figure instead of the original.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 100 (giving 0.25 instead of 25%).
  • Getting confused about which year is "original" when the question gives data for three years — always use the year stated in the question as your base.
Worked example
Revenue was £400,000 last year and £460,000 this year. What is the percentage change?
Step 1: Find the change
£460,000 − £400,000 = £60,000
Step 2: Divide by original
£60,000 ÷ £400,000 = 0.15
Step 3: Multiply by 100
0.15 × 100 = 15% increase
Index numbers
Index number = Value in current periodValue in base period × 100
Rules
  • The base period always has an index of 100.
  • An index above 100 means the value has increased since the base period.
  • An index below 100 means the value has decreased.
  • To find the percentage change from the base: subtract 100 from the index.
Common traps
  • Treating the index number as the actual value (an index of 120 does not mean £120).
  • Comparing index numbers from two different base years as if they are directly comparable.
  • Forgetting that you cannot calculate the actual values from index numbers alone unless you are given at least one real figure.
Worked example
Sales in Year 1 (base) were £200,000. Sales in Year 3 are £230,000. What is the index number for Year 3?
£230,000 ÷ £200,000 = 1.15
1.15 × 100 = 115

This means sales have increased by 15% compared to the base year.
3.1.2 Shares & dividends
1Market capitalisation
Market capitalisation = Number of issued shares × Current share price
What to know
This measures the market value of a company's issued equity — what the stock market thinks the company is worth. It is not the same as the total value of all the business's assets. Investors use it to compare the size of public limited companies.
Common traps
  • Using the nominal (face) value of shares instead of the current market price.
  • Confusing "issued shares" with "authorised shares" — use the number actually in circulation.
Worked example
A plc has 50 million shares in issue. The current share price is £4.20. What is its market capitalisation?
50,000,000 × £4.20 = £210,000,000 (£210m)
2Dividend per share
Dividend per share = Total dividend paidNumber of ordinary shares in issue
What to know
This is what each shareholder receives per share they own. A higher figure attracts investors; a lower figure may suggest the company is retaining profits for investment.
Common traps
  • Using profit for the year instead of the total dividend paid — they are not the same thing.
  • Watch the units. If total dividends are given in £millions and you need pence per share, convert carefully.
Worked example
A company pays a total dividend of £6 million. It has 20 million shares in issue.
£6,000,000 ÷ 20,000,000 = £0.30 (30p per share)
3Dividend yield %
Dividend yield (%) = Dividend per share (pence)Share price (pence) × 100
What to know
Dividend yield compares annual dividends per share with the share's current market price. It does not include capital gains or losses, and it is not necessarily the return on the investor's original purchase price. Investors compare it to interest rates on savings — a higher yield may make shares more attractive.
Common traps
  • Both values must be in the same unit — typically pence. If dividend per share is given in £ and share price in pence (or vice versa), convert before dividing.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 100.
Worked example
Dividend per share is 30p. Share price is 420p.
30 ÷ 420 = 0.0714…
0.0714 × 100 = 7.14%
3.1.3 Marketing data & demand
4Market share %
Market share (%) = Sales of one product / brand / businessTotal sales in the market × 100
What to know
Use the same measure for both — either sales revenue or sales volume, but not one of each. The question will usually make this clear.
Common traps
  • Mixing up sales value and sales volume.
  • Using the firm's total sales when the question asks about a single product or brand.
Worked example
A business has sales of £8 million in a market worth £50 million.
£8m ÷ £50m = 0.16
0.16 × 100 = 16%
5Market growth %
Market growth (%) = Change in the size of the market over a periodOriginal size of the market × 100
What to know
This is simply the percentage change formula applied to total market size. A growing market is generally more attractive for new entrants.
Common traps
  • Confusing market growth with sales growth — market growth is about the whole market, not just one firm.
  • Dividing by the new market size instead of the original.
Worked example
The market was worth £200m last year and £218m this year.
Change = £218m − £200m = £18m
£18m ÷ £200m = 0.09
0.09 × 100 = 9% market growth
6Price elasticity of demand (PED)
PED = % change in the quantity demanded% change in price
What to know
  • PED is normally negative because price and demand usually move in opposite directions. Some textbooks ignore the sign; AQA may give the value as negative.
  • If PED is between 0 and −1 → price inelastic (demand changes less than price).
  • If PED is beyond −1 (e.g. −2.5) → price elastic (demand changes more than price).
  • The specification says "calculate, interpret and analyse" — you may need to calculate PED from given data as well as interpret a value you are given.
Common traps
  • Saying demand is "elastic" when PED is −0.5 — that is inelastic.
  • Confusing which goes on top. It is always % change in quantity demanded on top, % change in price on the bottom.
  • Forgetting the business implication: if demand is price inelastic, raising the price increases total revenue — but only while demand remains inelastic over that price range.
Worked example
A firm raises its price by 10% and sees quantity demanded fall by 5%. What is the PED?
PED = −5% ÷ +10% = −0.5 (price inelastic)

Because PED is between 0 and −1, a price increase would increase total revenue — as long as demand remains price inelastic over the new price range.
7Income elasticity of demand (YED)
YED = % change in the quantity demanded% change in income
What to know
  • If YED is positive → normal good (demand rises as income rises).
  • If YED is negative → inferior good (demand falls as income rises, e.g. budget brands).
  • The higher the positive value, the more sensitive demand is to income changes — luxury goods tend to have YED well above +1.
Common traps
  • Confusing PED and YED — PED uses price changes, YED uses income changes.
  • Assuming all goods are normal goods — some products actually lose sales when customers get richer.
Worked example
Incomes rise by 8% and demand for a luxury brand increases by 20%.
YED = +20% ÷ +8% = +2.5 (income elastic, normal/luxury good)

The business is vulnerable to economic downturns — demand will fall sharply if incomes drop.
28Return on marketing spend %
Return on marketing spend (%) = Profit from marketing activitiesAmount of marketing spend × 100
What to know
This measures how effectively marketing money has been used. A result above 100 means the marketing generated more profit than it cost; below 100 means it did not pay for itself.
Common traps
  • Using revenue instead of profit — the formula specifically says profit from marketing activities.
  • Remember to multiply by 100 — AQA's formula includes × 100, so the result is a percentage figure.
Worked example
A social media campaign cost £20,000 and generated £55,000 in profit attributable to the campaign.
£55,000 ÷ £20,000 = 2.75
2.75 × 100 = 275

For every £1 spent on marketing, the business generated £2.75 in profit.
3.1.4 Break-even
8Variable costs (total)
Total variable costs = Variable cost per unit × Number of units sold
What to know
Variable costs change in direct proportion to output — materials, packaging, piece-rate labour. Fixed costs (rent, salaries) are not included.
Common traps
  • Including fixed costs in the variable cost figure.
  • Forgetting that "number of units sold" may differ from "number of units produced" if some are unsold.
Worked example
Variable cost per unit is £3.50 and the business sells 12,000 units.
£3.50 × 12,000 = £42,000
9Total costs
Total costs = Fixed costs + Variable costs
Worked example
Fixed costs = £80,000. Variable costs = £42,000.
£80,000 + £42,000 = £122,000
10Revenue
Revenue = Selling price per unit × Number of units sold
What to know
Revenue is sometimes called turnover or sales. It is the total income from selling goods or services before any costs are deducted.
Common traps
  • Confusing revenue with profit. Revenue does not account for any costs.
  • Using cost per unit instead of selling price.
Worked example
Selling price is £8 per unit and 12,000 units are sold.
£8 × 12,000 = £96,000
11Contribution per unit
Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit
What to know
Contribution is what each unit "contributes" towards covering fixed costs and then generating profit. It is not profit — profit only begins once all fixed costs have been covered.
Common traps
  • Subtracting total costs instead of just variable costs.
  • Confusing contribution with profit.
Worked example
Selling price = £8. Variable cost per unit = £3.50.
£8 − £3.50 = £4.50 contribution per unit
12Total contribution
Total contribution = Contribution per unit × Units sold
OR: Total revenue − Total variable costs
Worked example
Contribution per unit = £4.50. Units sold = 12,000.
£4.50 × 12,000 = £54,000
13Break-even output
Break-even output = Fixed costsContribution per unit
What to know
This tells you the number of units the business must sell to cover all its costs — no profit, no loss. The formula assumes that selling price, variable cost per unit and fixed costs remain constant over the relevant range of output.
Common traps
  • Dividing fixed costs by selling price instead of contribution per unit.
  • Dividing by total costs — the formula uses only fixed costs on top.
  • If the answer is not a whole number, always round up. You cannot sell half a unit, so break-even is the next whole number above.
Worked example
Fixed costs = £80,000. Contribution per unit = £4.50.
£80,000 ÷ £4.50 = 17,777.78
Break-even = 17,778 units (rounded up)
14Margin of safety
Margin of safety = Actual level of output − Break-even level of output
What to know
The margin of safety tells you how far sales can fall before the business starts making a loss. A larger margin of safety means less risk.
Common traps
  • Subtracting the wrong way round (break-even minus actual gives a negative number).
  • Not linking the result to risk — always explain what a high or low margin of safety means for the business.
Worked example
Actual output = 22,000 units. Break-even = 17,778 units.
22,000 − 17,778 = 4,222 units

The business could lose 4,222 sales before it starts making a loss.
3.1.4 Budgets
15Budget variance
Budget variance = Budgeted figure − Actual figure
What to know
  • The formula gives a positive or negative number, but you cannot decide favourable or adverse from the sign alone — it depends on the type of figure.
  • For revenue: actual higher than budgeted is favourable (even though the formula gives a negative number).
  • For costs: actual lower than budgeted is favourable (the formula gives a positive number).
  • Always state the size of the variance and label it favourable or adverse.
Common traps
  • Assuming a positive result is always favourable or a negative result is always adverse — it depends whether you are looking at revenue or costs.
  • If budgeted costs were £50,000 and actual costs were £55,000, the variance is −£5,000 (adverse) — costs were higher than planned.
  • Forgetting to label the variance as favourable or adverse.
Worked example
Budgeted revenue = £120,000. Actual revenue = £135,000.
Budgeted costs = £80,000. Actual costs = £88,000.
Revenue variance: £120,000 − £135,000 = −£15,000
But actual revenue is higher → this is a £15,000 favourable variance

Cost variance: £80,000 − £88,000 = −£8,000
Actual costs are higher → this is a £8,000 adverse variance
3.1.4 Cash flow & liquidity
16Payable days
Payable days = PayablesCost of sales × 365
What to know
This tells you how many days, on average, a business takes to pay its suppliers. A higher number means the business holds onto cash longer, but might damage supplier relationships.
Common traps
  • Using revenue instead of cost of sales on the bottom.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 365.
  • Confusing payable days with receivable days — they measure different things.
Worked example
Payables = £40,000. Cost of sales = £300,000.
£40,000 ÷ £300,000 = 0.1333
0.1333 × 365 = 48.7 days (≈ 49 days)
17Receivable days
Receivable days = ReceivablesRevenue × 365
What to know
This tells you how many days, on average, it takes customers to pay the business. A lower number is better for cash flow.
Common traps
  • Using cost of sales instead of revenue. Receivable days uses revenue; payable days uses cost of sales. They are different.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 365.
Worked example
Receivables = £55,000. Revenue = £500,000.
£55,000 ÷ £500,000 = 0.11
0.11 × 365 = 40.15 days (≈ 40 days)
18Current ratio
Current ratio = Current assetsCurrent liabilities
What to know
  • This measures short-term liquidity — can the business pay its debts due within a year?
  • A ratio of around 1.5–2 is often used as a rough guide, but the acceptable level varies by industry — a supermarket may operate well below 1.5 because it receives cash daily.
  • Below 1 means the business may struggle to pay its short-term debts.
  • Too high (e.g. 5:1) could mean the business has idle assets not being used productively.
Common traps
  • Putting current liabilities on top — it is current assets divided by current liabilities.
  • Expressing it as a percentage — it should be expressed as a ratio, e.g. 1.8:1.
Worked example
Current assets = £180,000. Current liabilities = £100,000.
£180,000 ÷ £100,000 = 1.8:1
19Acid test ratio
Acid test ratio = Current assets − InventoryCurrent liabilities
What to know
  • This is a stricter test than the current ratio because it removes inventory (which may be hard to sell quickly).
  • A ratio of around 1:1 is often used as a rough guide, but what is acceptable depends on the industry and how quickly the business can convert assets to cash.
  • Below 1:1 could signal liquidity problems, though some businesses (e.g. large retailers) operate normally at this level.
Common traps
  • Forgetting to subtract inventory from current assets before dividing.
  • Using the wrong figure for inventory — it should be the inventory figure from current assets on the statement of financial position.
Worked example
Current assets = £180,000. Inventory = £50,000. Current liabilities = £100,000.
(£180,000 − £50,000) ÷ £100,000
£130,000 ÷ £100,000 = 1.3:1
3.1.4 Profit & profitability
20Profit
Profit = Total revenue − Total costs
OR: Total contribution − Fixed costs
What to know
Both versions give the same answer. Use whichever fits the data you have been given.
Worked example
Total revenue = £96,000. Total costs = £122,000.
£96,000 − £122,000 = −£26,000 (a loss)
21Gross profit
Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales
What to know
Gross profit only deducts the direct costs of making or buying the goods sold. It does not include overheads like rent, marketing or admin.
Common traps
  • Using total costs instead of cost of sales — that would give you operating profit, not gross profit.
Worked example
Revenue = £500,000. Cost of sales = £300,000.
£500,000 − £300,000 = £200,000
22Gross profit margin %
Gross profit margin (%) = Gross profitRevenue × 100
What to know
This tells you how much of every £1 of revenue is left after paying the direct costs. A GPM of 40% means 40p from every £1 is gross profit.
Common traps
  • Dividing by cost of sales instead of revenue.
  • Confusing gross profit margin with operating profit margin.
Worked example
Gross profit = £200,000. Revenue = £500,000.
£200,000 ÷ £500,000 = 0.4
0.4 × 100 = 40%
23Operating profit
Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses
What to know
Operating expenses include rent, salaries, marketing, utilities — everything involved in running the business day to day. Operating profit shows how well the core business is performing.
Worked example
Gross profit = £200,000. Operating expenses = £140,000.
£200,000 − £140,000 = £60,000
24Operating profit margin %
Operating profit margin (%) = Operating profitRevenue × 100
Worked example
Operating profit = £60,000. Revenue = £500,000.
£60,000 ÷ £500,000 = 0.12
0.12 × 100 = 12%
25Profit for the year
Profit for year = Operating profit + Profit from other activities − Net finance costs − Tax
What to know
This is the final "bottom line" profit after everything has been deducted, including interest and tax. This is what can be distributed as dividends or retained.
Common traps
  • Forgetting to subtract both net finance costs and tax.
  • Confusing "profit from other activities" with revenue — it refers to non-operating income such as property rental or investment income.
Worked example
Operating profit = £60,000. Profit from other activities = £5,000. Net finance costs = £8,000. Tax = £11,400.
£60,000 + £5,000 − £8,000 − £11,400 = £45,600
26Profit for the year margin %
Profit for year margin (%) = Profit for yearRevenue × 100
Worked example
Profit for year = £45,600. Revenue = £500,000.
£45,600 ÷ £500,000 = 0.0912
0.0912 × 100 = 9.12%
3.1.4 Gearing, ROCE & ROI
27Return on capital employed (ROCE) %
ROCE (%) = Operating profitTotal equity + Non-current liabilities × 100
What to know
  • Total equity + non-current liabilities = capital employed.
  • ROCE tells you how efficiently a business uses its long-term capital to generate profit.
  • ROCE can be compared with the cost of borrowing — if it is consistently lower, this raises questions about whether the business is using its capital effectively. But a single year's figure does not prove value destruction.
Common traps
  • Using profit for the year instead of operating profit.
  • Using total assets instead of capital employed (total equity + non-current liabilities).
  • Forgetting what capital employed means — it appears in both the ROCE and gearing formulas.
Worked example
Operating profit = £60,000. Total equity = £200,000. Non-current liabilities = £100,000.
Capital employed = £200,000 + £100,000 = £300,000
£60,000 ÷ £300,000 = 0.2
0.2 × 100 = 20%
29Return on investment (ROI) %
ROI (%) = Profit from the investment (£)Cost of the investment (£) × 100
What to know
  • ROI measures the percentage return on a specific investment. A positive ROI means the investment generated profit; a negative ROI means it lost money.
  • "Profit from the investment" means the net gain — revenue generated minus all costs associated with the investment.
Common traps
  • Using total revenue from the investment instead of the profit from the investment.
  • Confusing ROI with ROCE — ROI is about a specific investment; ROCE is about the whole business.
  • Using a different version of the formula (e.g. gain minus cost over cost) — AQA's version uses "profit from the investment ÷ cost of the investment × 100".
Worked example
A business invests £50,000 in new equipment. It generates £12,000 profit from the investment.
£12,000 ÷ £50,000 = 0.24
0.24 × 100 = 24%
30Gearing %
Gearing (%) = Non-current liabilitiesTotal equity + Non-current liabilities × 100
What to know
  • Gearing shows what proportion of a business's long-term finance comes from borrowing.
  • 50% is a commonly used reference point — above it, the business relies more on debt than equity and is often described as highly geared. But there is no fixed danger threshold; what matters is whether the business can comfortably service its debt.
  • High gearing means higher interest payments, which is riskier if profits fall.
Common traps
  • Using current liabilities instead of non-current liabilities — gearing only looks at long-term debt.
  • Putting total equity on top instead of non-current liabilities.
  • Note: the denominator is the same as for ROCE (capital employed).
Worked example
Non-current liabilities = £100,000. Total equity = £200,000.
Capital employed = £200,000 + £100,000 = £300,000
£100,000 ÷ £300,000 = 0.333
0.333 × 100 = 33.3%

This business has relatively low gearing — most of its finance comes from equity rather than debt.
3.2 People & operations
31Employee productivity
Employee productivity = Output over a time periodNumber of employees
What to know
Output could be units produced, orders processed, or any measurable output. Higher productivity means the business is getting more from each worker.
Common traps
  • Confusing productivity with efficiency — productivity is per employee; efficiency can relate to other resources too.
  • Using revenue instead of output.
Worked example
A factory produces 240,000 units per year with 60 employees.
240,000 ÷ 60 = 4,000 units per employee per year
32Sales per employee
Sales per employee = Sales over a time periodNumber of employees
Worked example
Annual sales = £1.2 million. Number of employees = 15.
£1,200,000 ÷ 15 = £80,000 per employee
33Employee turnover %
Employee turnover (%) = Number of staff leavingNumber of staff employed by the business × 100
What to know
A high turnover rate can indicate problems such as poor pay, weak culture or limited progression — but it can also reflect seasonal employment, restructuring or deliberate replacement of underperforming staff. Some industries naturally have higher turnover (retail, hospitality). Always consider the context before judging.
Common traps
  • Using the number of new hires instead of the number of staff leaving.
  • Not considering context — a turnover of 30% might be normal in fast food but alarming in a law firm.
Worked example
12 employees left during the year. The business has 80 staff.
12 ÷ 80 = 0.15
0.15 × 100 = 15%
34Employee costs as a percentage of revenue %
Employee costs (%) = Employee costsRevenue × 100
What to know
This shows how much of each £1 of revenue goes on paying staff. Labour-intensive industries (e.g. education, healthcare) will naturally have higher figures.
Worked example
Employee costs = £320,000. Revenue = £800,000.
£320,000 ÷ £800,000 = 0.4
0.4 × 100 = 40%
35Unit costs (average costs)
Unit costs = Total costsNumber of units of output
What to know
Spreading fixed costs over more units tends to reduce unit costs — this is the basis of economies of scale. However, increasing output can also push up variable costs or lead to diseconomies of scale, so average costs do not always fall as output rises.
Common traps
  • Using only variable costs instead of total costs.
  • Forgetting to link the result to economies or diseconomies of scale in your analysis.
Worked example
Total costs = £250,000. Output = 50,000 units.
£250,000 ÷ 50,000 = £5.00 per unit
36Capacity utilisation %
Capacity utilisation (%) = Actual outputMaximum possible output × 100
What to know
  • 100% means operating at full capacity — no spare capacity.
  • Low capacity utilisation means fixed costs are spread over fewer units, raising unit costs.
  • Very high capacity utilisation (near 100%) makes it hard to respond to sudden increases in demand.
Common traps
  • Putting maximum output on top instead of actual output.
  • Not linking the answer to unit costs — this is usually the key analytical point.
Worked example
A factory produces 36,000 units per month. Maximum capacity is 48,000 units.
36,000 ÷ 48,000 = 0.75
0.75 × 100 = 75%

The factory has 25% spare capacity. Unit costs could be reduced if output increased.
37Inventory turnover
Inventory turnover = Cost of salesAverage inventories held
What to know
  • This tells you how many times the business sells and replaces its inventory in a year.
  • A higher number means faster-moving stock — generally a good sign, but an unusually high figure could indicate the business holds too little inventory and risks stockouts.
  • A very low number could mean slow-selling or obsolete stock.
Common traps
  • Using revenue instead of cost of sales.
  • Putting inventory on top — it is cost of sales ÷ average inventories, not the other way round.
  • This is expressed as "times" not as a percentage — do not multiply by 100.
Worked example
Cost of sales = £300,000. Average inventory = £25,000.
£300,000 ÷ £25,000 = 12 times per year

On average, the business sells and replaces its entire inventory every month.
38Float time (network analysis)
Float time = Latest Finish Time − Duration of activity − Earliest Start Time
What to know
  • Float tells you how long an activity can be delayed without delaying the whole project.
  • Activities on the critical path have a float of zero.
  • Activities with float give managers flexibility in scheduling and resource allocation.
Common traps
  • Getting the order of subtraction wrong — it is LFT minus duration minus EST, not the other way round.
  • Confusing EST and LFT with activity start and end times.
  • Saying an activity is on the critical path when it has float > 0.
Worked example
Activity C: EST = 4 days, LFT = 12 days, Duration = 5 days.
Float = 12 − 5 − 4 = 3 days

Activity C can be delayed by up to 3 days without affecting the project completion date.
3.3.3 Investment appraisal
39Payback period
Step 1: Identify the net cash flows for each period
Step 2: Keep a cumulative total (including the initial investment as a negative)
Step 3: When cumulative cash flow becomes positive = end of payback

For the precise payback point:
Amount still to pay back at start of final year ÷ Cash flow in that year
What to know
  • Payback measures how long it takes to get the initial investment back.
  • Shorter payback = lower risk, but it ignores what happens after the payback point.
  • It does not consider the time value of money (unlike NPV).
Common traps
  • Forgetting that the initial investment is a negative cash flow in Year 0.
  • Not calculating the precise payback period when the question asks for it — you will need to work out the fraction of a year. Converting this to months (e.g. 0.6 × 12 = 7.2 months) assumes cash flows arrive evenly during the year.
  • Mixing up net cash flow with cumulative cash flow.
Worked example
Initial investment = £100,000. Annual net cash flows: Year 1 = £30,000, Year 2 = £40,000, Year 3 = £50,000, Year 4 = £35,000.
Cumulative cash flows:
Year 0: −£100,000
Year 1: −£100,000 + £30,000 = −£70,000
Year 2: −£70,000 + £40,000 = −£30,000
Year 3: −£30,000 + £50,000 = +£20,000 ← payback occurs here

Precise payback:
Amount needed at start of Year 3: £30,000
Cash flow in Year 3: £50,000
£30,000 ÷ £50,000 = 0.6 of a year
Payback = 2 years and 0.6 years (2 years 7 months)
40Average rate of return (ARR) %
ARR (%) = Average annual return (£)Initial cost of project (£) × 100
What to know
  • Average annual return = (total net cash flows − initial cost) ÷ number of years. You must subtract the initial investment before dividing by the number of years — this gives you the average annual profit, not the average annual cash flow.
  • ARR can be compared directly with interest rates or other investments.
  • It considers the whole life of the project (unlike payback).
Common traps
  • Not calculating the average annual return correctly — you need total net returns over the whole project divided by the number of years.
  • Confusing ARR with ROCE — ARR is for a specific project, ROCE is for the whole business.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 100.
Worked example
Initial investment = £100,000. Total net cash flows over 4 years = £155,000.
Step 1: Total net return (profit)
£155,000 − £100,000 = £55,000
Step 2: Average annual return
£55,000 ÷ 4 years = £13,750
Step 3: ARR
£13,750 ÷ £100,000 = 0.1375
0.1375 × 100 = 13.75%
41Net present value (NPV)
Present value = Cash flow × Discount factor

NPV = Total of all present values − Cost of investment
What to know
  • NPV accounts for the time value of money — £1 today is worth more than £1 in the future.
  • A positive NPV means the project is expected to add value at the chosen discount rate — it supports the case for going ahead, but is not the only factor a business would consider.
  • A negative NPV means the project is expected to destroy value at that discount rate.
  • Discount factors will be given in the question — you do not need to calculate them.
Common traps
  • Forgetting to apply the discount factor to every year's cash flow, including Year 0 (although Year 0's discount factor is usually 1.000).
  • Adding up the present values without subtracting the initial investment.
  • Comparing NPV figures from projects of different durations without thinking about the timescale.
  • Double deduction: if Year 0 is already included in the table as a negative present value (−£100,000 × 1.000), do not subtract the investment again at the end. Either include Year 0 in the sum, or exclude it and subtract separately — never both.
Worked example
Initial investment = £100,000. Discount rate = 8%.

The NPV is positive at an 8% discount rate, which supports the case for the investment. However, the business should also consider the reliability of the cash flow forecasts, the risk involved, strategic fit and whether better alternatives exist.
EXAM The own-figure rule

In AQA Business exams, if you make an error in an early part of a calculation but then use your (wrong) figure correctly in the rest of the working, you can still earn marks for the later steps. This is the own-figure rule.

This means:

  • Always show your full working, even if you are not confident in your answer.
  • If you get the wrong contribution per unit but then use it correctly in the break-even formula, you still earn credit for the break-even method.
  • Never leave a calculation blank — a method mark is still a mark.
