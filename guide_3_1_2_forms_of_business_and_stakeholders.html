3.1.2 Different Forms of Business

Sole traders

Definition
A sole trader is a business owned and run by one person. The owner has complete control over the business but bears unlimited liability — meaning there is no legal distinction between the owner and the business. If the business cannot pay its debts, the owner’s personal assets (house, car, savings) can be used to pay creditors.

Sole traders are the simplest form of business to set up. There are no registration fees (other than registering for self-assessment with HMRC) and no legal requirement to publish accounts. The owner keeps all the profits after tax. However, raising finance is limited to personal savings, bank loans, and retained profits. Growth is constrained by the owner’s own resources and time.

Private limited companies (Ltd)

Definition
A private limited company (Ltd) is a business with a separate legal identity from its owners. Shareholders own the company and benefit from limited liability — they can only lose the amount they invested, not their personal assets. Shares cannot be sold to the general public.

Ltd companies must register with Companies House and file annual accounts, although these can be abbreviated for small companies. The business can raise finance by selling shares to family, friends, employees, or private investors — but not on the stock exchange. Control stays with the existing shareholders, who can choose who to sell shares to.

Example
Gymshark Ltd started as Ben Francis’s sole trader business but incorporated as a private limited company to raise investment. Ben retained around 70% of shares, General Atlantic (a private equity firm) held 20%+, and senior managers owned around 10%. This allowed Gymshark to raise £200 million for expansion while Ben kept majority control.

Public limited companies (plc)

Definition
A public limited company (plc) is a company whose shares are available for the general public to buy and sell on a stock exchange. Shareholders benefit from limited liability. The company must have a minimum share capital of £50,000.

Going public (an IPO — initial public offering) allows a business to raise very large amounts of capital by selling shares to the public. However, it brings significant obligations: the company must publish full annual accounts, hold an AGM (annual general meeting), and comply with stock exchange regulations. Importantly, the original founders can lose control — anyone can buy shares, and if enough shares change hands, the founders may become minority shareholders.

PLCs face constant pressure from shareholders to deliver dividends and increase the share price. This can lead to short-term decision-making — cutting costs to meet quarterly profit expectations rather than investing in long-term growth.

Example
Apple Inc. is listed on the NASDAQ stock exchange. Its share price is influenced by investor expectations about future products and profits. In the SAM Paper 1 case study, Apple’s share price rose 48% in 2023, partly because investors expected growth from products like Vision Pro. But the share price is constantly judged by the market — if Apple missed profit forecasts, the share price would fall regardless of the company’s long-term potential.

Co-operatives

The specification names three types of co-operative: worker, consumer, and producer.

Definition
A co-operative is a business owned and democratically controlled by its members, who share in the benefits. Each member typically has one vote, regardless of how much they have invested. The purpose is to serve the members’ interests rather than to maximise profit for external shareholders.

Key point
Co-operatives have different objectives from companies. Farmoor Cycles (SAM Paper 3) has a mission that includes “promoting cycling” and “building a fairer society through cooperative ownership.” Its values are democracy, honesty, openness, social responsibility, and caring for others. These objectives would change fundamentally if the business became a private limited company — this is exactly what SAM Paper 3 Q2.4 tests.

Social enterprises

Definition
A social enterprise is a business that trades to fulfil a social or environmental purpose. It generates revenue like any other business, but the majority of its surplus (profit) is reinvested into its social mission rather than distributed to shareholders or owners.

Social enterprises are not charities. They sell products or services in the market and must be commercially viable to survive. The difference is that profit is a means to achieve the social purpose, not the purpose itself.

Common confusion
Students often confuse social enterprises with charities. A charity relies primarily on donations and grants. A social enterprise earns its revenue through trade, like any business. It must compete in the market, manage costs, and generate enough surplus to sustain its operations and fund its social mission.
Example
Madlug is a social enterprise that sells bags and luggage. For every bag sold, another bag is donated to a child in the care system. The business model is commercial — it designs, manufactures, and sells bags at market prices. But its purpose is social — ensuring that children in care do not have to carry their belongings in bin bags when they move between homes.

Why the form of business matters

The form of business is not just a legal technicality. It shapes everything about how the business operates. A sole trader faces different pressures from a plc. A co-operative makes decisions differently from a company run by a single entrepreneur. The AQA teaching guide makes this point clearly: the form of business underpins all decisions and strategic direction.

Evaluation point
The most important comparison is often between who has control and what the business can access. An Ltd gives the founder limited liability and access to more finance, but they must share ownership. A plc gives access to huge amounts of capital, but the founder may lose control entirely. A co-operative ensures democratic decision-making, but growth may be slower because each new member has equal say. There is always a trade-off.

Reasons to buy shares

People buy shares for two main reasons:

Dividends — A share of the company’s profits, paid out regularly (usually twice a year). This provides an income stream. Some investors — particularly pension funds — depend on dividend income.

Capital growth — If the share price rises above what the investor paid, they can sell the shares for a profit. This is a capital gain. Investors in growth companies like Gymshark or early-stage tech firms are usually more interested in capital growth than dividends.

Some investors also buy shares for voting rights (influence over company decisions) or because they believe in the company’s mission.

3.1.2 Shares and Share Price

What influences share price

Share prices are determined by supply and demand on the stock exchange. If more people want to buy a company’s shares than sell them, the price rises. If more people want to sell than buy, the price falls. But what drives that supply and demand?

FactorHow it affects share price
Expected future profitsIf investors believe the company will be more profitable in the future, demand for shares increases and the price rises. If they expect profits to fall, the price drops.
Dividend paymentsA company that pays reliable, growing dividends is attractive to investors. A dividend cut signals trouble and usually causes the share price to fall.
Market conditionsA booming economy generally pushes share prices up because consumer spending and business profits tend to rise. A recession has the opposite effect.
Company newsProduct launches, new contracts, changes in leadership, or legal problems all affect investor confidence and therefore share price.
Industry trendsIf the industry a company operates in is growing (e.g. AI, renewable energy), share prices in that sector tend to rise. A declining industry pulls prices down.
Interest ratesHigher interest rates make saving in a bank more attractive relative to investing in shares, reducing demand. Higher rates also increase borrowing costs for businesses, reducing expected profits.
Investor sentimentSometimes share prices move on confidence or fear rather than fundamentals. A social media rumour or analyst downgrade can cause a sell-off.

Significance of share price changes

Changes in share price matter for several reasons:

For the company: A falling share price makes it harder to raise new capital by issuing shares (investors will pay less per share). It can also make the company vulnerable to a takeover — if the share price is low, another company can buy a controlling stake cheaply. A rising share price does the opposite — it makes issuing new shares more attractive and deters hostile takeovers.

For shareholders: A rising share price increases the value of their investment. A falling price reduces it. For shareholders who depend on dividend income, a falling share price combined with a dividend cut is particularly damaging.

For the business’s reputation: Share price is often used as a measure of how well the business is performing. A sustained fall can damage confidence among customers, suppliers, and employees.

Example — SAM Paper 1
Apple’s share price rose 48% in 2023. The SAM asks students to analyse one factor that might have caused this. Possible factors include: investor confidence in new products like Vision Pro, strong iPhone sales, Apple’s $100 billion+ cash reserves signalling stability, and the broader tech sector rally driven by AI excitement. The key is to pick one factor and develop a chain of reasoning showing how it leads to increased demand for shares and therefore a higher price.
3.1.2 Calculations — Market Capitalisation, Dividends, Dividend Yield

Market capitalisation

Formula
Market capitalisation = Current share price × Number of shares issued

Market capitalisation tells you the total market value of a company — what investors collectively think the business is worth. It is a snapshot that changes whenever the share price moves.

Worked example
A company has 500 million shares in issue. The current share price is £4.20.

Market capitalisation = £4.20 × 500,000,000 = £2.1 billion

If the share price rises to £5.00, the market capitalisation becomes £5.00 × 500,000,000 = £2.5 billion. The company’s underlying business has not changed — but the market’s valuation of it has increased by £400 million because of higher investor confidence.
Interpretation
Market capitalisation does not tell you what a company is “really” worth — it tells you what the market thinks it is worth at this moment. A company might have a high market capitalisation because investors are optimistic about its future, even if its current profits are modest. Equally, a profitable company might have a low market capitalisation if investors are worried about its future.

Dividends per share

Formula
Dividends per share = Total dividends paid ÷ Number of shares issued

This tells you how much each shareholder receives per share they own. Companies decide how much profit to distribute as dividends and how much to retain for reinvestment. There is always a trade-off — higher dividends please shareholders but leave less for the business to invest in growth.

Worked example
A company pays out £30 million in dividends. There are 500 million shares in issue.

Dividends per share = £30,000,000 ÷ 500,000,000 = £0.06 (6p per share)

A shareholder who owns 10,000 shares would receive 10,000 × £0.06 = £600 in dividends.

Dividend yield

Formula
Dividend yield (%) = (Dividends per share ÷ Current share price) × 100

Dividend yield is the return an investor gets on their investment through dividends alone (it ignores capital growth). It allows investors to compare the income they would receive from different companies’ shares, or to compare shares with other investments like savings accounts.

Worked example
Dividends per share = £0.06. Current share price = £4.20.

Dividend yield = (£0.06 ÷ £4.20) × 100 = 1.43%

This means an investor receives 1.43% of the value of their shares back each year as dividends. If a bank savings account offers 4.5% interest, the dividend yield alone makes the shares look less attractive — but the investor may also expect capital growth if the share price rises.
Evaluation point
A low dividend yield does not necessarily mean a bad investment. Growth companies like Amazon and Tesla have historically paid little or no dividends because they reinvest profits into expansion. Investors buy these shares for capital growth, not income. Conversely, a very high dividend yield can be a warning sign — it might mean the share price has fallen sharply (making the yield look high) because investors are worried about the company’s future.
3.1.2 Stakeholders
Definition
A stakeholder is any individual or group that is affected by, or has an interest in, the activities and decisions of a business. Stakeholders are different from shareholders — shareholders own part of the company, while stakeholders is a broader term that includes shareholders but also employees, customers, suppliers, the community, and government.
Common confusion
Students frequently confuse stakeholders with shareholders. A shareholder is a specific type of stakeholder — someone who owns shares in the company. But most stakeholders (employees, customers, suppliers, the community) are not shareholders. In a 9-mark or 15-mark question, showing that you understand this distinction is important.

Internal stakeholders

Employees

Employees have a stake in the business because it provides their income, working conditions, job security, and career development. Their interests include fair pay, safe working conditions, job stability, and opportunities for progression. Decisions that threaten these interests — such as redundancies, pay freezes, or relocation — will be opposed by employees.

Owners / shareholders

Owners want the business to succeed and grow in value. For shareholders in a plc, this means dividends and share price growth. For the owner of a sole trader business, it means personal profit. Owner interests can conflict with other stakeholders — for example, cutting costs to increase profit might involve reducing employee benefits or using cheaper (but less ethical) suppliers.

External stakeholders

Customers

Customers want good quality products at fair prices, reliable service, and honest marketing. They are affected by pricing decisions, product changes, and the business’s ethical behaviour. If a business acts unethically, customers may boycott it.

Suppliers

Suppliers want reliable orders, prompt payment, and a fair price for their goods or services. A large business that uses its bargaining power to squeeze suppliers on price or extend payment terms can put suppliers under financial pressure.

Other creditors

Creditors (e.g. banks that have lent money) want the business to repay its debts on time. They have a financial interest in the business remaining solvent. If the business takes on too much risk, creditors become nervous.

Community

The local community is affected by the business’s operations — employment, noise, traffic, pollution, and the use of local resources. A factory closure can devastate a local economy. A business expanding and creating jobs benefits the community.

Government

The government has an interest in businesses paying taxes, complying with regulations (employment law, health and safety, environmental standards), and contributing to economic growth. Government policy (tax rates, minimum wage, trade regulations) directly affects businesses.

Impact of business activity on stakeholders

Business decisions rarely affect just one stakeholder group. A decision that benefits shareholders (e.g. cutting costs to increase dividends) might harm employees (e.g. redundancies) or customers (e.g. lower quality). This creates stakeholder conflict.

Example — SAM Paper 3
Farmoor Cycles is a worker co-operative considering becoming a private limited company. This decision would affect every stakeholder group differently:

Employees: In a co-operative, employees are the owners — they have decision-making power, share profits, and shape the business’s values. Converting to an Ltd means they lose this power. They become employees, not owners. Decisions would be made by directors and shareholders, not the workforce.

Customers: Farmoor’s mission includes providing affordable bicycles. A private company driven by profit might raise prices to improve margins, which would harm customers who rely on Farmoor for affordable transport.

Suppliers: A larger, better-funded Ltd might place bigger orders (good for suppliers) but could also use its buying power to negotiate harder on price or extend payment terms (bad for suppliers).

Ethical responsibilities to stakeholders

Businesses have legal obligations to stakeholders (paying the minimum wage, meeting health and safety standards, paying taxes). But ethical responsibilities go further — they involve doing the right thing even when the law does not require it.

Examples include: paying employees a living wage rather than just the minimum wage; sourcing materials sustainably even when cheaper alternatives exist; being transparent with customers about product ingredients or sourcing; investing in the local community beyond what is legally required.

Evaluation point
Whether a business meets its ethical responsibilities often depends on its form of ownership. A social enterprise has ethical responsibility built into its purpose. A co-operative’s democratic structure means member interests are protected. A plc under pressure to maximise shareholder returns may find it harder to justify ethical spending that reduces short-term profit — even if it is better for the business in the long run. This links directly back to forms of business.

Traps Common Mistakes on 3.1.2
1. Confusing stakeholders with shareholders
A shareholder is one type of stakeholder — someone who owns shares. Stakeholders include everyone affected by the business: employees, customers, suppliers, the community, and government. In an exam, be precise about which group you are discussing.
2. Describing forms of business instead of comparing them
A common weak answer lists the features of an Ltd and then lists the features of a plc. The question usually asks you to compare — explain how they differ on a specific issue (control, finance, liability) and why this matters for the business in the case study.
3. Treating social enterprises as charities
Social enterprises earn revenue through trade. They sell products or services like any business. The difference is that surplus is reinvested into a social mission rather than distributed to shareholders. A charity relies primarily on donations and grants.
4. Forgetting that share price is about supply and demand
Students often list reasons why a share price might change without connecting them to supply and demand. Every factor works through the same mechanism: it changes how many people want to buy or sell the shares. Make this explicit in your analysis.
5. Assuming all shareholders want the same thing
Institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies) often want steady dividends. Individual investors might prefer capital growth. A private equity firm wants to increase the value of the company and sell its stake at a profit within a few years. The type of shareholder affects what the business is pressured to do.
6. One-sided stakeholder answers
A question about stakeholder conflict needs both sides. If employees oppose a decision, explain why — but also explain why other stakeholders (shareholders, customers) might support it. Then make a judgement about whose interests carry more weight and why.

6-mark Analyse: “Analyse one factor that might have caused this change in Apple’s share price.” [6 marks] — SAM Paper 1 Q2.2

Model answer

One factor that might have caused the change in Apple’s share price is investor expectations about future profits, because share prices are determined by supply and demand — when investors believe a company will be more profitable in the future, demand for its shares increases, pushing the price up. Apple spent $30 billion on research and development in 2023, which was significantly more than any competitor. During this period, Apple announced Vision Pro, its first major new product category since the Apple Watch, and the product was positioned as the future of spatial computing. This would have signalled to investors that Apple was investing heavily in innovation that could open entirely new revenue streams, which means that investors would expect higher profits in the future as these products reach the market. As a result, more investors would want to buy Apple shares to benefit from expected future dividends and capital growth, which would increase demand for the shares relative to supply on the NASDAQ stock exchange, therefore driving the share price up. This helps explain why Apple’s share price rose 48% in a single year — the market was pricing in not just Apple’s current profits but its anticipated future revenue from new products and categories.

What makes this work
One factor, clearly identified and rooted in the theory (supply and demand for shares). Applied to the case study using specific data (£30 billion R&D, Vision Pro, 48% share price rise). A chain of analysis with connectives throughout. The chain ends at the question’s endpoint — why the share price changed.

9-mark Assess: “Assess whether Farmoor Cycles’ stakeholders would be likely to oppose the business becoming a private limited company.” [9 marks] — SAM Paper 3 Q2.4

Structure — FOR → AGAINST → DECIDE
Each argument paragraph needs: case study data including manipulated data woven throughout → at least five chains of analysis using connectives (this means that, as a result, therefore, this could lead to, this is because) reaching the endpoint → business models or theories applied where relevant.

The weighted judgement follows a fixed formula: Overall… → Although… carries greater weight because… → However, this ultimately depends on… → Therefore [final sentence answering the question in the words of the question].

Step 1 — Argument FOR (stakeholders would oppose becoming an Ltd)

Argument for

On one hand, one could argue that Farmoor Cycles’ stakeholders would be likely to oppose the business becoming a private limited company. In a worker co-operative, employees are the owners — they have decision-making power through democratic voting (one member, one vote) and share in the profits generated by the business. Farmoor Cycles operates across three shops in Oxford, Birmingham and Manchester, and its values include democracy, honesty, openness, social responsibility, and caring for others. This means that converting to an Ltd would fundamentally change the relationship between the employees and the business, because they would move from being owners with voting rights to being employees whose role is determined by directors and shareholders. Using stakeholder mapping, the employees would shift from being ‘key players’ (high power, high interest) to ‘keep informed’ (low power, high interest) — they would still care deeply about the business but would lose the power to shape its direction. As a result, employees might fear that the new owners would prioritise profit over Farmoor’s social mission, which could lead to higher prices for customers who currently rely on Farmoor for affordable bicycles. Farmoor’s stated mission includes “promoting cycling” and “building a fairer society through cooperative ownership” — and therefore the very purpose of the business would be at risk if it became a company driven by shareholder returns rather than member benefit. This shows that stakeholders, particularly employees and customers who value the cooperative model, would likely oppose a change that threatens the values and democratic structure that define Farmoor Cycles.

Step 2 — Argument AGAINST (stakeholders would not oppose becoming an Ltd)

Argument against

On the other hand, one might argue that Farmoor Cycles’ stakeholders would not oppose the business becoming a private limited company. Converting to an Ltd would give Farmoor access to significantly more sources of finance — the company could sell shares to private investors, attract bank lending more easily (because limited liability reduces lender risk), and potentially bring in private equity investment. Farmoor currently operates three shops and is considering investing in a new inventory management system. As a cooperative funded by member contributions and retained surplus, the business may struggle to fund both the technology investment and further expansion simultaneously. This means that becoming an Ltd could enable Farmoor to grow faster — opening new shops, investing in technology, and building a stronger online presence — which could lead to higher revenue and more stable long-term employment for the workforce. As a result, even employees who value the cooperative model might accept the change if it means their jobs are more secure and their pay is funded by a more financially robust business. This is because Farmoor’s three shops across different cities require consistent investment in stock, maintenance and marketing, and a cooperative that cannot raise sufficient capital risks stagnation or decline. From a stakeholder vs shareholder perspective, the key question is whether the additional investment that Ltd status could unlock would ultimately benefit all stakeholders — employees through job security, customers through better service, and suppliers through larger, more reliable orders — even if the democratic ownership structure is lost. This could further lead to suppliers benefiting from bigger, more predictable orders as the business expands, which would give them more certainty in their own planning. This shows that stakeholders may not oppose the change if they believe the financial benefits of becoming an Ltd outweigh the loss of cooperative values.

Step 3 — Weighted judgement

Weighted judgement

Overall, Farmoor Cycles’ stakeholders would be likely to oppose the business becoming a private limited company, but the strength of this opposition would vary between stakeholder groups. Although the financial argument for becoming an Ltd is significant — the business could access more capital, grow faster, and offer employees greater job security — the argument for opposition carries greater weight because Farmoor’s identity is built on cooperative ownership, and its values of democracy, social responsibility and fairness are not just additions to the business but its defining features. Employees who chose to work at a cooperative did so partly because of these values, and losing them would change the nature of the employment relationship in a way that no amount of additional investment can compensate for. However, this ultimately depends on which stakeholder group has the most influence — if a small number of members with the most to gain financially from the conversion push for the change, they could use their votes to override the majority, particularly if they argue that the cooperative cannot survive without more investment. Therefore, Farmoor Cycles’ stakeholders would be likely to oppose becoming a private limited company because the loss of democratic control and cooperative values would directly contradict the mission and principles that stakeholders — especially employees — value most about the business.

Why this is Level 3 (7–9 marks)
AO1: Knowledge of cooperative structure, Ltd features, stakeholder theory, limited liability, sources of finance — demonstrated through the argument rather than separate definitions.
AO2: Data used and manipulated throughout: three shops (Oxford, Birmingham, Manchester), values (democracy, honesty, openness, social responsibility, caring for others), mission (“promoting cycling”, “building a fairer society through cooperative ownership”), inventory system investment, member contributions.
AO3: Both paragraphs have at least five chains of analysis using connectives (this means that, because, as a result, which could lead to, this is because, therefore, this shows that). Both chains reach the endpoint — whether stakeholders would oppose the change.
AO4: The judgement commits to a position (stakeholders would oppose), acknowledges the counterargument (financial benefits are significant), explains why one side carries more weight (cooperative values define the business), adds a condition (“depends on which stakeholder group has the most influence”), and the final sentence answers the question using the words of the question.
Sophisticated concepts: Stakeholder mapping (power vs interest grid) and stakeholder vs shareholder approaches are woven into the analysis naturally.

Opportunity costAnalytical conceptEvery decision about form of business has an opportunity cost. Staying as a sole trader means keeping control but giving up the finance that incorporation would bring.
How to use them in your answers
Weave sophisticated concepts into your analysis naturally. For example, instead of writing “Stakeholder mapping is a model that classifies stakeholders by power and interest...” and then applying it, simply write: “Using stakeholder mapping, employees of Farmoor Cycles would move from being key players with high power and high interest to a keep-informed position, because converting to an Ltd would remove their voting rights while their interest in business decisions remains just as high.”
Context Real-World Examples for 3.1.2
Real-world examples
Ltd — keeping control while raising capital: Gymshark Ltd raised £200 million by selling a minority stake to General Atlantic (private equity). Ben Francis retained around 70% of shares, meaning he kept majority control. This shows that an Ltd can raise substantial capital without the founder losing control — but the trade-off is that the private equity firm will eventually want a return on its investment, which could mean floating on the stock exchange or selling the business.

Plc — share price pressure: Apple’s share price rose 48% in 2023. But plcs face constant pressure — if Apple missed a quarterly earnings forecast, the share price could fall significantly in a single day. Tim Cook must balance long-term investment (like the $30 billion R&D spend) against short-term shareholder expectations for dividends and profit growth.

Co-operative — democratic but slow: The Co-operative Group is owned by its members (customers). Major decisions require member approval, which can slow decision-making. In 2013, the Co-op Bank nearly collapsed partly because its governance structure made it difficult to respond quickly to financial problems. This illustrates a key disadvantage of cooperative ownership.

Social enterprise — trading with purpose: Madlug sells bags and donates a bag to a child in care for every sale. The business must be commercially competitive — its bags compete with mainstream brands on quality and price — while directing its surplus to its social mission. This is the defining feature of a social enterprise: it trades to fulfil a purpose, not to maximise shareholder returns.

Stakeholder conflict — Farmoor Cycles (SAM): The SAM Paper 3 asks whether Farmoor Cycles’ stakeholders would oppose becoming an Ltd. This is a direct test of stakeholder analysis — employees lose decision-making power, customers may face higher prices, but the business could access more finance for growth. The answer depends on which stakeholder group’s interests are given priority.

Why businesses stay Ltd rather than float: The AQA teaching guide notes that Gymshark chose to remain as an Ltd rather than floating on the stock exchange. This preserves Ben Francis’s control over the brand and business direction, avoids the disclosure requirements of being a plc, and protects the business from short-term share price pressure. The trade-off is that Gymshark cannot raise capital as easily as a plc.
Evaluation Judgement Phrases for 3.1.2
Useful phrases for evaluation
“Whether this is the right form of business depends on the owner’s priorities — if control matters more than rapid growth, staying as an Ltd makes sense; if the business needs large-scale investment, floating as a plc may be necessary despite the loss of control.”

“The most significant stakeholder group in this decision is arguably [group], because they have the most to lose and the least ability to influence the outcome.”

“On balance, the stakeholder approach is more appropriate for this business because its competitive advantage depends on trust and reputation, which would be damaged by prioritising short-term shareholder returns.”

“This ultimately depends on the time horizon — in the short term, shareholders benefit from cost-cutting, but in the long term, the damage to employee morale and customer loyalty could reduce profitability.”

“The strength of stakeholder opposition depends on whether they have realistic alternatives — employees who can easily find other jobs are less likely to oppose a change than those with limited options in the local labour market.”

“A falling share price does not necessarily mean the business is performing badly — it may simply reflect market sentiment or broader economic conditions rather than anything the company has done.”
Summary Exam Reminders for 3.1.2
Things to remember
1. The five forms of business on the spec are: sole traders, private limited companies (Ltd), public limited companies (plc), co-operatives (worker, consumer, producer), and social enterprises. Know the key differences between them.

2. The seven comparison issues are: control, objectives, sources of finance, distribution of profits, unlimited and limited liability, reasons to buy shares, and influences on share price. Be able to compare any two forms of business on these issues.

3. Social enterprises are not charities. They trade commercially but reinvest surplus into a social or environmental mission.

4. Share price is determined by supply and demand. When analysing factors that cause share price changes, always connect the factor back to how it affects the number of buyers and sellers.

5. Know the three calculations: Market capitalisation = share price × number of shares. Dividends per share = total dividends ÷ number of shares. Dividend yield = (dividends per share ÷ share price) × 100.

6. Stakeholders are not the same as shareholders. Shareholders are one type of stakeholder. Internal stakeholders: employees, owners/shareholders. External stakeholders: customers, suppliers, other creditors, community, government.

7. The form of business shapes everything — objectives, finance, decision-making, stakeholder relationships. It is not just a standalone topic; it underpins the entire specification.

8. In 6-mark analyse questions, pick one factor and develop a chain of reasoning. Use connectives to build the chain. End at the question’s endpoint.

9. In 9-mark assess questions, use: FOR → AGAINST → weighted judgement. Weave case study data and business models into both arguments. The judgement must come down on one side with a reason.

10. Stakeholder mapping (power vs interest grid) is an Annex 8 sophisticated concept. Use it to strengthen your analysis in stakeholder questions — but weave it in naturally rather than defining it first.
