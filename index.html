Paper 1

How the exam works

Paper 1 structure

Paper 1 is 2 hours, 90 marks, 33.3% of the A-level. It contains two case studies. Each case study is followed by five compulsory questions worth 45 marks.

In AQA’s current sample paper, each case study contains two 6-mark Analyse questions, two 9-mark Assess questions and one 15-mark Evaluate question.

Unit 3.1 is the direct content for Paper 1, but this knowledge can also be drawn on synoptically in Papers 2 and 3.

6 marks — Analyse

AO1 = 2, AO2 = 2, AO3 = 2. No AO4.

One well-developed argument can get full marks. No conclusion, balance or evaluation is required.

The thinking: concept → context → mechanism → precise endpoint.

Endpoint rule

If the question asks about profit, do not stop at sales. If it asks about market share, do not merely reach sales. If it asks about PED, discuss sensitivity to price rather than just demand. AQA defines strong analysis as a chain with the appropriate starting point and endpoint.

9 marks — Assess

AO1 = 2, AO2 = 2, AO3 = 2, AO4 = 3. Three levels: Level 3 (7–9), Level 2 (4–6), Level 1 (1–3).

Level 3 requires: knowledge explained to show understanding, good application to context, logical chains of reasoning, and evaluation which effectively weighs up arguments and reaches a convincing, coherent judgement that fully addresses the question.

There is no compulsory structure. Students may argue for and against then conclude, evaluate throughout, or open with a judgement and support it. But for Level 3 there must be a clear answer to the question with a weighing up of argument and counter-argument.

From AQA’s mark scheme guidance

The 9-mark question requires a decision on a particular issue — it does not require students to consider other issues. A response that lacks any evaluation can receive a maximum of 6 marks. A response with AO1 and AO3 but no context or judgement can receive a maximum of 4 marks.

15 marks — Evaluate and recommend

AO1 = 3, AO2 = 3, AO3 = 4, AO4 = 5. Five levels: Level 5 (13–15), Level 4 (10–12), Level 3 (7–9), Level 2 (4–6), Level 1 (1–3).

Students will normally evaluate two options and recommend one.

Level 5 requires: precise, well-selected knowledge, application throughout, well-developed logical chains for both options (using sophisticated concepts where appropriate), and evaluation that weighs up both options, provides a coherent overarching judgement and fully addresses the question.

The recommendation should explain why the chosen option is preferable to the alternative in this specific situation — not just repeat an earlier argument.

Quantitative questions

Papers 1 and 2 each contain two quantitative questions (9 or 15-mark). Six marks within each are linked to quantitative skills:

  • AO1 (2 marks) — knowing the formula and selecting the right numbers
  • AO2 (2 marks) — performing the calculation
  • AO3 (1 mark) — interpreting and using the result in an argument
  • AO4 (1 mark) — using the findings to support a judgement

There are an additional 3 marks for further qualitative analysis and evaluation in relation to the calculation.

Own-figure rule

An incorrect calculation can still earn analysis and evaluation credit if the student correctly interprets their own (wrong) result. So always write the interpretation even if you are not confident in the number.

3.1.1

Business and objectives

Entrepreneurs

Reasons for starting a business

Financial: profit, income, wealth creation, financial independence.

Non-financial: being their own boss, pursuing a passion, making a difference, flexibility, personal challenge, solving a problem, social impact.

Context matters

Do not assume every entrepreneur is motivated by maximising profit. A social entrepreneur may prioritise social objectives. A lifestyle entrepreneur may value independence. A founder backed by investors may face greater pressure for growth and financial returns. Use the case study to identify which motives apply.

Characteristics

The spec names six: risk taker, resilient, focused, passionate, innovative, adaptable.

CharacteristicWhat it meansHow to use it in analysis
Risk takerCommits resources despite uncertaintyOpportunity identified → resources invested → new product/market entered → potential increase in sales/success
ResilientRecovers from setbacksEarly failure → entrepreneur persists → refines approach → eventual success
FocusedMaintains attention on key objectivesAvoids distractions → resources directed efficiently → faster progress
PassionateStrong commitment and enthusiasmGreater effort → persuasive leadership → enthusiasm communicates to customers/investors
InnovativeIntroduces new ideas, products or processesInnovation → differentiation → customer value → increased demand
AdaptableResponds effectively when conditions changeMarket shifts → entrepreneur adjusts strategy → business remains competitive
AQA Paper 1 SAM Q1.1

Analyse how one entrepreneurial characteristic has contributed to the success of Gymshark Ltd. [6]

AQA expects one characteristic, supported by Gymshark context, with a clear link to a measure of success such as sales or profit. The top-scoring sample response uses risk-taking (dropping out of university) and reaches success through brand awareness, sales and investor confidence — all in one paragraph.

Challenges of starting a business

Difficulty obtaining finance, generating ideas, high barriers to entry, lack of experience, cash flow problems, competition from established businesses.

AQA encourages students to connect characteristics to challenges. For example: lack of finance + resilience → entrepreneur continues seeking funding → eventually secures investment → business can launch.

Change in role as business grows

In a start-up, the entrepreneur personally develops products, handles marketing, manages finance and sells to customers. As the business grows, AQA highlights a shift towards leading teams, delegating tasks and coordinating resources.

Trap

Do not write that the entrepreneur “is no longer important.” Their role changes — it does not disappear. They move from doing everything to managing the people who do it.

Business planning and decisions

Business plans

A business plan sets out what the business intends to do and how. It typically covers objectives, product/service, target market, marketing approach, operations and financial forecasts.

Its value: provides direction, coordinates decisions, identifies resource requirements, anticipates problems, supports finance applications, gives targets to measure progress against.

Its limitations: depends on the quality of assumptions and accuracy of forecasts. A detailed plan based on poor sales forecasts may create false confidence. The value also depends on how rapidly the market changes and whether managers actually use and update it.

Competitiveness

The ability of a business or product to perform against rivals. This goes beyond price — it includes quality, service, differentiation, efficiency, innovation, brand and customer experience.

Trap

Do not reduce competitiveness to “charging lower prices.” A premium brand can be highly competitive without being the cheapest.

Influences on business decisions

The spec lists seven: objectives, risk, reward, resources, market conditions, ethics, opportunity cost.

Resources constrain decisions — money, people, skills, time, capacity and technology. A theoretically attractive strategy can be inappropriate if the business lacks the resources to carry it out.

Opportunity cost is particularly useful in evaluation. Choosing one action means sacrificing the next best alternative. A strong evaluation might say: “The real issue may not simply be whether Option A generates a benefit, but whether those resources could generate a greater benefit elsewhere.”

Risk, reward and ethical dilemmas

Decisions offering greater potential returns may carry greater uncertainty. Students should consider probability, scale of downside, possible upside and the business’s ability to absorb failure.

AQA specifically highlights the profit vs ethics dilemma. Ethically sourced inputs may raise costs and reduce short-term margins — but ethical sourcing may also improve reputation, build trust, strengthen demand and increase long-term revenue. The trade-off depends on the business’s objectives and ownership structure.

Ethical does not mean legal

Something can be legal but ethically questionable. AQA explicitly highlights this confusion. Ethical issues are about what is right, not just what is allowed.

Business objectives and SMART

Objectives provide targets and guide decision-making. Effective objectives are:

Specific — clear outcome. Measurable — progress can be quantified. Accountable — someone is responsible for achieving it. Realistic — achievable with available resources. Time specific — deadline attached.

Trap

AQA uses Accountable, not “Achievable” which you may have learned at GCSE. Make sure you use the correct specification term.

SMART as a critique tool

Do not just expand the acronym. Use SMART to evaluate whether an objective is actually useful. “Increase sales” is not SMART. “Increase UK sales by 10% within 12 months” is. AQA suggests SMART can be used to critique objectives — for example, identifying that an objective without a time dimension may be less effective.

Exam tip

Objectives underpin evaluation in 9 and 15-mark questions. A business with a growth objective will make different decisions from one focused on survival. When you recommend a course of action, link it back to the business’s objectives.

3.1.2

Forms of business and stakeholders

Forms of business

The exam will not ask you to list the features of a sole trader. It will ask you to analyse or assess whether a particular business should change its form, or what the consequences of its current form are.

FormOwnershipLiabilityKey issues
Sole traderOne personUnlimitedFull control and profit, but personal assets at risk, limited finance options
Private LtdShareholders (restricted)LimitedMore finance options, continuity, but ownership can be diluted, more legal requirements
Public plcShareholders (public)LimitedLarge-scale finance via stock exchange, but pressure from shareholders for dividends and short-term results
Co-operativeMembers (workers, consumers or producers)Depends on legal structureDemocratic control, shared returns, but can have capital-raising difficulties, potentially slower decisions
Social enterpriseVariesVariesTrades like a normal business but surplus reinvested into social/environmental mission
The form determines what decisions are available

A sole trader cannot issue shares. A plc must publish its accounts. A social enterprise reinvests its surplus. When a case study tells you the form of the business, think about what that means for the question being asked.

Trap

Social enterprise ≠ charity. AQA explicitly warns about this. A social enterprise trades as a business and can have financial objectives. The difference is that surplus is reinvested to support its social purpose.

Gymshark link

Gymshark in the SAM remained a private Ltd even after bringing in General Atlantic. Ben retained 70% ownership. This makes control highly relevant to finance and ownership decisions.

Shares, dividends and market capitalisation

Market capitalisation = Issued shares × Current share price

Dividend per share = Total dividend paid ÷ Number of ordinary shares in issue

Dividend yield (%) = (Dividend per share ÷ Share price) × 100

Trap

Market capitalisation is not the same as revenue, profit or total assets. It is the market’s valuation of the company’s equity at a point in time.

Why people buy shares

Dividend income, potential capital gain if the share price rises, and ownership/voting rights.

What affects share price?

Expected future profits, recent performance, dividends, investor confidence, major announcements, economic conditions. The key word is expectations — markets react to what investors believe will happen, not only what has already happened.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q2.2

Analyse one factor that might have caused a change in Apple’s share price. [6]

The chain must reach share price: factor → investor expectations → demand for shares changes relative to supply → share price rises/falls. Do not stop at “Apple’s profit increased.”

Stakeholders

Internal: employees, owners/shareholders. External: customers, suppliers, other creditors, community, government.

Trap

A shareholder is a stakeholder. Not every stakeholder is a shareholder. AQA specifically identifies this as a common confusion.

Stakeholder conflict

Employees want fair pay and job security. Shareholders want dividends and a rising share price. Customers want quality at reasonable prices. Suppliers want prompt payment. These interests often conflict.

Which stakeholder matters most depends on power, urgency, the business’s objectives, its ownership structure, its financial position and market conditions.

Where the analysis lives

Identifying that “stakeholders are affected” is not enough. Explain how a specific group is affected and why their interests conflict with another group’s.

Ethical responsibilities to stakeholders

Businesses have ethical responsibilities to each stakeholder group — paying fair wages, being honest with customers, treating suppliers reasonably, minimising harm to communities. Whether a business acts on these often depends on its form, its objectives and the pressure it faces.

3.1.3

Marketing management

Market research

Market research gathers information to reduce uncertainty surrounding marketing decisions. It does not eliminate risk — it reduces it.

Primary vs secondary

Primary: new data collected specifically for the business’s purpose (surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation). Current and specific, but expensive and time-consuming. Poor samples can produce unreliable results.

Secondary: existing data originally gathered for another purpose (government statistics, industry reports, competitor data). Quicker and cheaper, but may be outdated, may not directly fit the firm’s needs, and the original method may be unknown.

Quantitative vs qualitative

Quantitative: numerical data — useful for identifying patterns, calculating percentages, comparing groups, estimating market size. Qualitative: opinions, attitudes, motivations — useful for understanding why consumers behave as they do.

Trap

Quantitative does not automatically mean “better.” The research method must fit the question being investigated.

Confidence levels and intervals

A confidence level indicates how confident the researcher can be that the true population value lies within the calculated interval. A confidence interval is the statistical range itself (e.g. ±4%).

Worked example

A survey finds that 68% of smartwatch users would consider a health-monitoring app, with a confidence interval of ±4% at a 95% confidence level. This means we can be 95% confident that the true figure for the population is between 64% and 72%.

Key points

Sample-based research cannot provide absolute certainty. A narrower interval gives greater precision. Increasing the confidence level normally widens the interval, other things being equal. AQA identifies this as an area students find challenging.

Market mapping

Places brands/products on axes using customer-relevant characteristics (e.g. low price ↔ high price, basic ↔ premium). Purpose: identify gaps, understand competitive positioning, support differentiation.

Trap

A gap on a market map does not automatically represent a profitable opportunity. There may be no demand there.

Influences on how research is conducted

Risk (how important is the decision?), cost (budget available?), time (how quickly is the answer needed?), need for reliability (how confident does the business need to be?).

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q2.3

Assess whether an increase in spending on market research could be profitable for Apple. [9]

For: better research → better understanding of demand → marketing mix better matched to customers → fewer unsuccessful launches → increased profit.

Against: Apple already has extensive customer data from 2 billion devices → marginal gain may be small → extra cost and delay → competitors may move first.

Judgement: depends on whether additional research improves decisions enough to generate benefits exceeding its cost and delay.

Markets and marketing data

Types of markets

B2B (business to business) — fewer but higher-value customers, longer relationships, more personal selling. B2C (business to consumer) — larger customer base, greater role for mass/digital promotion. C2C (consumer to consumer) — individuals transacting via platforms (e.g. Vinted, eBay).

Goods vs services

Services are intangible and often consumed at the same time they are delivered, making employee behaviour, consistency and customer experience particularly important. This affects marketing decisions — you cannot photograph a service or let a customer try it before purchase in the same way.

Market size, growth and share

Market share (%) = (Business sales ÷ Total market sales) × 100

Market growth (%) = (Change in market size ÷ Original market size) × 100

Market size is the total value or volume of sales in a market. It can be measured in units (volume) or revenue (value). You may need to calculate the size of individual market segments within a larger market.

Critical trap

Sales growth ≠ market-share growth. If a business’s sales rise by 5% but the market grows by 15%, the business has actually lost market share.

Sales data, volume and value

Volume = number of units sold. Value = monetary worth of those sales. A company can have falling volume but increasing sales value if prices rise. The spec requires you to interpret sales data by region, product and store.

Index numbers, percentages and percentage changes

AQA explicitly requires analysis using index numbers, percentage calculations and percentage changes. An index number shows change relative to a base year (base = 100). If an index rises to 115, the value has increased by 15% since the base year.

Correlation

Positive (variables move together), negative (move in opposite directions), strong or weak.

Golden rule

Correlation does not prove causation. There may be coincidence, reverse causality, or another variable affecting both.

Return on marketing spend

Return on marketing spend (%) = (Profit from marketing activities ÷ Amount of marketing spend) × 100

Trap

A campaign with large sales may not have the highest return if it also costs far more. This measures efficiency, not just scale.

Demand and elasticity

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

PED is normally negative (price up, demand down) but we focus on the magnitude.

If |PED| is…Demand is…Effect of a price increase on revenue
Greater than 1Price elasticRevenue falls — quantity drops proportionately more than price rises
Less than 1Price inelasticRevenue rises — quantity drops proportionately less than price rises
Equal to 1Unit elasticRevenue stays the same
Worked example

A business increases its price by 10%. Quantity demanded falls by 5%. PED = −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5 (inelastic). Because |PED| < 1, the percentage fall in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage rise in price — so total revenue increases.

The profit trap

PED tells you what happens to revenue, not profit. Higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit — profit also depends on costs. AQA explicitly warns about this. If the question asks about profit, you must consider both revenue and costs.

Factors affecting PED

Demand tends to be less price sensitive where: branding/loyalty is strong, substitutes are limited, the item is a small proportion of income, consumers perceive it as important or necessary.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q1.2

Analyse one way in which having a brand as strong as Nike’s could affect the PED for Gymshark’s products. [6]

Strong brand → greater loyalty → fewer perceived substitutes → customers less likely to switch after a price increase → quantity demanded falls proportionately less → demand becomes relatively more price inelastic. Stopping at “sales increase” does not answer the PED endpoint.

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income

If YED is…The product is…When incomes rise…
Positive and > 1Luxury / income elasticDemand rises more than proportionately
Positive but < 1Normal / income inelasticDemand rises but less than proportionately
NegativeInferior goodDemand falls — consumers switch to preferred alternatives
Calculation example

YED = +2. Incomes rise by 4%. Expected change in quantity demanded: +8%.

Trap

Negative YED does not mean the product is bad. Budget supermarkets, value clothing and public transport all have negative YED — when people earn more, they switch to alternatives they prefer.

Target market and segmentation

Market segmentation divides a market into groups of consumers with similar characteristics.

Demographic (age, income, gender, occupation), geographic (region, country, climate), psychographic (lifestyle, attitudes, values), behavioural (purchase habits, loyalty, usage rate).

Choosing and entering a target market

Influenced by: size and growth of the segment, customer needs, level of competition, business resources, potential profitability, and fit with the brand and capabilities.

Niche vs mass markets

NicheMass
SizeSmall, specialisedLarge, broad
CompetitionOften less directIntense
PricesOften premiumOften lower (economies of scale)
Key riskReliance on a narrow customer baseIntense competition and potentially greater resource requirements
No inherently “better” option

It depends on resources, brand, objectives, competition and capabilities. The exam wants you to consider which is more appropriate for this business.

Adapting the marketing mix for the target market

Once a target market is chosen, the business should adapt its product, price, place and promotion to match the needs and characteristics of that segment. A premium niche segment requires a different mix from a mass-market value segment.

Marketing objectives and planning

The spec lists: lead generation, sales, market share, brand awareness, customer retention, engagement, conversion.

Distinctions that matter

Engagement ≠ sales. Awareness ≠ conversion. Sales growth ≠ market-share growth. These distinctions are highly examinable.

Influences on marketing objectives

Corporate objectives, market conditions, competition, available resources, stage of product lifecycle, regulatory environment, and the business’s form and ownership. A plc facing shareholder pressure may prioritise short-term sales targets; a social enterprise may prioritise brand awareness within its community.

Marketing budget

Affected by objectives, resources, expected returns, competitors, stage of product lifecycle, market conditions. More marketing expenditure is worthwhile only if the incremental benefit exceeds the incremental cost.

Changes in demand

Seasonal — predictable variation during the year. Cyclical — varies with the wider economic cycle. Rising demand may justify higher promotion, higher inventory, additional distribution, possibly higher prices. Falling demand may lead to promotions, price reductions, repositioning or product changes.

Interrelationship with other functions

Marketing decisions affect and are affected by finance (budget constraints), operations (production capacity) and HR (staffing). For example, a marketing campaign that doubles orders is pointless if operations cannot fulfil them. Strong answers recognise these connections.

How marketing decisions affect competitiveness

Better segmentation → more targeted offer → stronger customer loyalty → harder for competitors to attract those customers. Strong branding → reduced price sensitivity → ability to charge higher prices while maintaining demand. Effective digital marketing → lower customer acquisition cost → improved profitability relative to competitors.

Marketing mix: product

Product lifecycle

Development → costs incurred, no sales. Introduction → low sales, high promotional needs, often negative profit. Growth → rapid sales increase, competitors may enter. Maturity → sales growth slows, competition intensifies. Decline → sales fall.

Don’t just describe the stages

AQA wants their significance for marketing planning and other functions. For example: maturity → slower sales growth → extension strategy needed → increased marketing expenditure → affects finance function.

Extension strategies

Advertising, rebranding, lowering the price, adapting the product. Purpose: extend maturity or slow decline.

Boston Matrix

High market shareLow market share
High growthStar — generates revenue but needs investmentQuestion mark — potential but uncertain
Low growthCash cow — strong cash flow, little investment neededDog — low revenue, candidate for review
Trap

“Dogs should always be sold off” is too simplistic. A dog may contribute to covering fixed costs or complete a product range. The Boston Matrix simplifies reality and ignores many qualitative factors.

New product development

Benefits: new revenue, access to growth markets, differentiation, replacing declining products. Challenges: high cost, uncertain demand, cannibalisation risk, long development periods, failure risk.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q2.4

Assess whether a large investment in NPD is likely to increase Apple’s market share. [9]

Keep the endpoint on market share, not merely sales. AQA rewards recognising that Apple’s existing portfolio may reduce the need for extremely high investment, and that what matters is how effectively the investment is used.

Price has knock-on effects

Price affects revenue, profit margins and brand perception simultaneously. Cutting the price might increase volume but reduce margins and damage a premium image.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q2.1

Analyse one way in which setting a high price might affect the revenue Apple earns. [6]

Positive route: strong brand → inelastic demand → price effect outweighs volume loss → revenue increases.

Negative route: Vision Pro far above competitors → customers switch/don’t purchase → quantity falls substantially → revenue decreases.

Either direction can score full marks if supported.

Marketing mix: distribution

Direct distribution: greater control, retain intermediary margin, direct customer data — but own logistics and infrastructure needed.

Intermediaries (wholesalers/retailers): wider reach, established distribution — but reduced control and margin goes to the intermediary.

E-commerce: lower overheads, wider reach, 24/7 — but intense competition, delivery logistics, customers cannot physically inspect products.

Multi-channel: increases reach and convenience — but adds complexity, cost and potential channel conflict.

Marketing mix: promotional mix

  • Advertising — paid messages through media
  • Social media and influencers — leveraging platforms and online personalities
  • Sales promotions — short-term incentives (discounts, competitions, BOGOF)
  • Sponsorship — association with an event, team or individual
  • Personal selling — direct contact, including account managers managing client relationships (particularly important in B2B)
  • Branding — building brand loyalty. Own-label brands are products manufactured for a retailer and sold under the retailer’s name rather than the manufacturer’s — they allow the retailer to control pricing and margins while offering a lower-cost alternative to branded goods.

Influences on the promotional mix

Target market, marketing objectives, budget, type of product, competition, stage of product lifecycle. A new product at introduction may need heavy advertising; a mature product may rely more on sales promotions and loyalty schemes.

Match the method to the business

A B2B industrial supplier is unlikely to benefit from TikTok influencers. A youth clothing brand is unlikely to benefit from trade magazine advertising. Always consider whether the method fits the target market.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q1.3

Assess whether increasing spending on influencers would lead to higher profits for Gymshark Ltd. [9]

Strong conclusion logic: “Increasing expenditure is likely to increase profit only if the extra contribution from Gymshark’s social-media audience exceeds the additional influencer costs. Given Gymshark already has 18 million followers and Ben himself has promotional reach, the marginal benefit of additional spending is what matters.”

Digital technology and marketing

This is a major new area on the 7138 spec.

Digital marketing methods

  • Social media campaigns — targeting, engagement, real-time communication, measurable
  • Content marketing — blogs, videos, articles designed to build trust and spread
  • SEO — optimising a website to rank higher in search results → more relevant traffic → more conversions
  • PPC — business pays each time someone clicks an ad. Clicks are not sales — high cost-per-click + low conversion = poor return
  • Website cookies — track user behaviour to personalise the experience and optimise sales

Measures of digital marketing success

Search performance, click-through rates, time on site, conversion rates, number of users/subscribers, advertising revenue.

Don’t interpret one metric alone

High traffic + low conversion may indicate ineffective targeting or poor website performance. Connect metrics to a business outcome.

CRM (Customer Relationship Management)

Supports: customer acquisition (gaining new customers), customer retention (keeping existing ones), lead generation (attracting potential customers), pipeline management (tracking potential sales through the process). AQA highlights particular relevance for B2B firms, small firms where replacing customers is expensive, and competitive markets.

Data analytics and AI in marketing

Segmentation (who to target), campaign performance tracking (what is working), personalisation (tailoring experience), predictions (forecasting trends), dynamic pricing (adjusting prices based on data).

How digital technology affects competitiveness

Better customer data → more accurate personalisation → higher conversion and retention → greater customer lifetime value → stronger competitive position. This is listed separately on the spec because AQA wants students to make this connection explicitly.

Trap

Data quality matters. Poor or incomplete data combined with sophisticated AI produces sophisticated poor decisions.

International marketing (A-level only)

Purpose, value and challenges of marketing internationally.

Challenges include language barriers, cultural differences, religious differences and understanding unfamiliar markets.

Adapting the marketing mix: product (tastes, features, packaging), price (income levels, competition, exchange rates), place (infrastructure, distribution habits), promotion (language, culture, media use).

Trap

International marketing ≠ just translating the adverts. AQA’s teaching guide specifically identifies language, cultural and religious differences as challenges.

Ethical issues in marketing (A-level only)

Ethical issues in product decisions (planned obsolescence, unsafe products), promotional decisions (misleading advertising, targeting vulnerable groups), pricing decisions (exploitative pricing, dynamic pricing that takes advantage of urgency).

3.1.4

Financial management

Managing finance

The spec lists five financial objectives: profit, profitability, cash flow, liquidity, levels of borrowing.

Influences on financial objectives

The type of business, its stage of development, market conditions, owners’ attitudes to risk, competitor performance and the objectives of other functions. A start-up typically prioritises cash flow and survival; an established plc may focus on profitability and shareholder returns.

Profit vs profitability

Profit is an absolute monetary amount. Profitability considers profit relative to another figure such as revenue or capital employed. Firm A with £10m profit may be less profitable than Firm B with £5m profit if Firm A’s revenue is far higher.

Cash flow vs profit

A profitable business can still run out of cash because customers have not yet paid, cash is tied up in inventory, or loan repayments require immediate cash. AQA identifies this as a common area of difficulty.

Interrelationship with other functions

Financial decisions affect and are affected by every other function. Marketing expansion increases finance requirements. Operations investment requires funding approval. Cutting HR spending may improve short-term profit but damage productivity. In the exam, recognise these connections — do not treat finance in isolation.

How financial decisions affect competitiveness

Securing the right finance at the right cost enables investment in capacity, technology, marketing and people. Poor financial management — running out of cash, excessive borrowing, underinvestment — weakens the business relative to competitors.

Match the source to the need

Short-term needs (covering a temporary shortfall) suit short-term finance (overdraft, trade credit). Long-term needs (buying equipment, expanding) suit long-term finance (loans, share capital, private equity). AQA’s teaching guide specifically recommends this distinction. Always explain why a source is suitable for this specific business.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q1.5

Evaluate two sources of external finance that Ben could use to fund Gymshark’s ambitious plans and recommend which one he should choose. [15]

AQA says: do not credit internal finance; overdraft is inappropriate for the scale implied; top responses must be rooted in Gymshark’s ambitious expansion; control is relevant because Ben retains 70%.

Loan: no dilution → Ben keeps control. But interest and repayments increase fixed cash outflows → greater financial risk if expansion underperforms.

Share capital / PE: large sums available + expertise. But ownership diluted → Ben sacrifices control.

Recommendation: depends on size of finance required, expected cash flow, and Ben’s priority regarding control.

Break-even

Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit

Total contribution = Contribution per unit × Units sold

Break-even output = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit

Margin of safety = Actual output − Break-even output

Worked example

Selling price = £10. Variable costs = £4/unit. Fixed costs = £30,000.

Contribution/unit = £10 − £4 = £6

Break-even = £30,000 ÷ £6 = 5,000 units

If actual sales = 7,000: Margin of safety = 7,000 − 5,000 = 2,000 units

What changes break-even?

Higher selling price → higher contribution → lower break-even. Higher variable costs → lower contribution → higher break-even. Higher fixed costs → higher break-even (contribution unchanged).

Break-even charts

You need to be able to interpret charts and illustrate the effects of changes. The key lines:

  • A horizontal fixed-cost line (stays the same regardless of output)
  • A total-cost line starting at fixed costs and rising with output (fixed + variable)
  • A revenue line starting at the origin and rising with output
  • The break-even point is where the revenue line crosses the total-cost line
  • Below that point: loss. Above: profit
  • The margin of safety is the horizontal distance between actual output and break-even output

If fixed costs rise, the total-cost line shifts upward and break-even output increases. If the selling price rises, the revenue line gets steeper and break-even output falls. If variable costs rise, the total-cost line gets steeper and break-even output increases.

Break-even is the minimum, not the target

A business wants to exceed break-even by as much as possible. In the exam, go further: what does the margin of safety tell us? Is the business at risk? What would happen if costs increased or prices fell?

Limitations

Break-even assumes one product, constant selling price, stable variable costs per unit, and that all output is sold. AQA’s teaching guide specifically notes difficulty where firms sell multiple products. Mentioning these limitations is a straightforward way to add evaluation.

Profit

Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales

Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses

Profit for year = Operating profit + Other income − Net finance costs − Tax

Gross profit margin (%) = (Gross profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Operating profit margin (%) = (Operating profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Profit for year margin (%) = (Profit for year ÷ Revenue) × 100

Increasing profit

Two routes: increase revenue or reduce costs. But every action has consequences. Price increase → higher revenue per unit, but may reduce demand. Cost reduction → stronger margins, but may reduce quality or service.

Always compare margins, not just profit

“The business made £1m profit” tells you nothing without context. Compare it to revenue (margin), to the previous year (trend), or to competitors (benchmarking).

Budgets

Purpose and value of budgeting

Target setting, planning, coordination across functions, resource allocation, monitoring and control, identifying variances, motivating managers by giving them financial targets and accountability.

Limitations

Based on forecasts which may be inaccurate, can create inflexibility, managers may build in “budgetary slack” (deliberately underestimating revenue or overestimating costs to make targets easier), time-consuming to prepare.

Zero-based budgeting

Every item starts at zero and must be justified from scratch, rather than basing next year’s budget on last year’s. Avoids “budget creep” but is time-consuming and managers may struggle to justify longer-term or intangible activities.

Budget variance = Budgeted figure − Actual figure

Favourable: actual result was better than expected. Adverse: worse than expected.

Trap

A favourable variance is not always good news. If the business spent much less than budgeted on marketing, that is a favourable cost variance — but it might mean marketing was underfunded and sales suffered. A variance is not favourable merely because the number is positive — for costs versus revenue, the interpretation differs. Consider why the variance occurred.

Cash flow and liquidity

Cash-flow forecasting

A cash-flow forecast estimates expected inflows and outflows over a future period. The structure:

Opening balance + Total inflows − Total outflows = Closing balance

The closing balance of one month becomes the opening balance of the next.

Purpose and value: identifies when cash shortfalls are likely to occur, allows the business to arrange finance in advance, helps plan the timing of major payments, supports applications for finance by showing when cash will be available.

Reading a cash-flow forecast

If the forecast shows a negative closing balance in Month 3, the business knows it needs to arrange finance (perhaps an overdraft) before that point. If receivables are high but cash is low, the issue may be slow-paying customers rather than weak sales.

You need to be able to complete cash-flow forecasts (fill in missing figures), interpret them (identify problems and causes), and analyse them (explain what the figures mean for the business).

Receivables and payables

Receivables = money owed to the business by customers. Payables = money the business owes to suppliers.

Trap

Students regularly reverse receivables and payables. AQA specifically identifies this confusion.

Improving cash flow

  • Debt factoring — selling receivables to a third party for immediate cash, but at a discount (you receive less than the full invoice value)
  • Shortening customer payment periods — receive cash earlier, but customers may dislike less generous credit
  • Early-payment incentives — offering a small discount for prompt payment
  • Credit checks — reduce the risk of customers not paying at all
  • Lengthening supplier payment periods — hold cash longer, but may damage supplier relationships
  • Reducing outgoings — cutting costs, but must consider impact on quality/capacity
  • Better invoice management — chasing payments promptly and accurately

Liquidity ratios

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities

Acid test ratio = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities

Payable days = (Payables ÷ Cost of sales) × 365

Receivable days = (Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365

The acid test is the stricter measure

It strips out inventory because inventory cannot always be quickly converted to cash. A current ratio of 2:1 might look healthy, but if most current assets are unsold stock, the acid test ratio will tell a very different story.

AQA Paper 1 SAM Q1.4

Assess whether Gymshark Ltd’s current ratio indicates the business was in a strong financial position in 2024. [9]

Gymshark’s result is 0.74 versus a clothing industry average of 1.4. Current assets are lower than current liabilities — a possible liquidity concern. But the current ratio only measures liquidity — it tells you nothing directly about profitability, long-term returns or the reasons behind the figures.

AQA says: an answer based solely on the calculation can score no more than 6. The result must be interpreted and evaluated.

Financial reporting

Income statement structure

Students must be able to interpret and amend income statements. The basic structure:

Revenue
− Cost of sales
= Gross profitPerformance relating to production/purchasing
− Operating expenses
= Operating profitPerformance from normal operations
± Other income/costs, − Finance costs, − Tax
= Profit for the yearFinal profit after everything

Statement of financial position (balance sheet)

Shows what the business owns, owes and is worth at a specific point in time:

Non-current assetsLong-term assets (property, equipment, vehicles)
Current assetsShort-term assets (inventory, receivables, cash)
Current liabilitiesShort-term debts (payables, overdraft)
Non-current liabilitiesLong-term debts (loans, mortgages)
EquityWhat belongs to shareholders (share capital + retained profit)

Key ratios

ROCE (%) = (Operating profit ÷ Capital employed) × 100

where Capital employed = Total equity + Non-current liabilities

Return on investment (%) = (Profit from investment ÷ Cost of investment) × 100

Gearing (%) = (Non-current liabilities ÷ Capital employed) × 100

ROCE is an important overall profitability measure

It shows how effectively the business uses its capital to generate profit. A declining ROCE, even if absolute profit is rising, suggests the business is becoming less efficient. But it should not be judged in isolation — always consider alongside liquidity, gearing and other measures.

Gearing

Higher gearing means a greater proportion of capital employed is financed through non-current liabilities. This increases financial risk because interest payments must be made regardless of profit. But borrowing can finance profitable growth without diluting ownership.

Trap

High gearing is not automatically bad. Whether it creates concern depends on the business’s cash flow, profitability, interest rates, objectives and industry. A stable, profitable business with predictable cash flows can handle higher gearing than a volatile start-up.

Limitations of financial reporting

Financial reports are historic (backward-looking), do not include non-financial factors (employee morale, brand reputation, environmental impact), and can be influenced by accounting choices.

Assessing financial position and performance

This is an explicitly separate spec heading. AQA expects students to bring together multiple measures and judge them relative to objectives, competitors, business planning and context.

The measures to integrate: profits and margins, ROCE, ROI, cash flow and liquidity, gearing, shareholder rewards (dividend per share, dividend yield), break-even output, margin of safety, budget variance.

Never judge from one number

The strongest answers triangulate. For example:

High operating margin + strong ROCE + weak current ratio + high gearing = a business that is profitable but may carry liquidity and financial-risk concerns.

The ratio is meaningless without a comparison — to previous years, competitors, targets or the industry average.

Exam guidance

When you calculate any financial ratio, your analysis should answer three questions: (1) What does the number mean? (2) Is it good or bad — compared to what? (3) What should the business do about it?

Ethics in finance (A-level only)

Two areas: tax avoidance and payment terms for customers and suppliers.

Tax avoidance means legally arranging business affairs to reduce tax liability. Tax evasion means deliberately not paying tax that is owed — this is illegal. The spec covers avoidance — the ethical question is whether a business should minimise its tax even though it legally can.

Payment terms: a large business demanding very long payment terms from small suppliers may preserve its own cash flow but place financial pressure on those suppliers. Similarly, offering customers very generous payment terms may win business but weaken the firm’s own cash position.

Evaluation territory

Delaying supplier payments → preserves cash → but may damage relationships → suppliers may eventually refuse favourable terms or prioritise other customers. AQA links these ethical choices to objectives and ownership structure.

Traps, formulae and question map

The 20 biggest Unit 3.1 exam traps

  1. Sales ≠ profit. Always account for costs when the endpoint is profit.
  2. Sales growth ≠ market-share growth. Compare business growth with market growth.
  3. Revenue ≠ cash flow. Revenue may be earned before payment arrives.
  4. Profit ≠ cash. A profitable business can run out of cash.
  5. Profit ≠ profitability. Compare margins, not just absolute figures.
  6. Operating profit ≠ profit for the year. They measure different things.
  7. Shareholder ≠ stakeholder. Shareholders are one stakeholder group.
  8. Social enterprise ≠ charity. Social enterprises trade for profit but reinvest surplus.
  9. Ethical ≠ legal. Something can be legal but ethically questionable.
  10. Correlation ≠ causation. Two variables moving together does not prove one causes the other.
  11. Brand strength → PED must reach price sensitivity, not just “more sales.”
  12. Market-share questions must reach market share, not simply sales.
  13. Revenue questions must reach revenue, not profit (unless the question asks for it).
  14. A quantitative answer ≠ calculation only. Interpret and use the figure.
  15. Ratios are meaningless without context. Always compare to something.
  16. More marketing spend ≠ more profit. Analyse marginal costs and returns.
  17. More market research ≠ better decisions. Quality and use of research matter.
  18. More NPD spend ≠ guaranteed innovation. Effectiveness matters more than expenditure.
  19. High gearing is not automatically bad. Context matters.
  20. An action being beneficial is not enough for a 15-marker. Explain why it is preferable to the alternative.
